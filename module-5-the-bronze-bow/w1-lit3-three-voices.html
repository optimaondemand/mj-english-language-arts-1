Lesson 5.03
Three Voices and a First Sight of Jesus
Literature · Week 1
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A character can fight an enemy outside and a battle inside at the same time.
Daniel hears three answers to one question: how should Israel be free? Rosh says fight by force. Rabbi Hezron says wait and keep the Law. A teacher named Jesus says something Daniel does not expect. A struggle a character has with the world is an external conflict. A struggle inside the character's own mind is an internal conflict. Today you learn to tell them apart in Daniel.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to tell an external conflict from an internal conflict in the story.
2
I will be able to describe how Daniel reacts to Jesus the first time he hears him.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
The Bronze Bow, Chapter 8 (and look back at the dinner argument in Chapter 5). (Read from your own copy of the book.)
⚡ WARM-UP · CHECK IN FIRST
When several people give you very different advice about a hard problem, what do you usually do? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THE LESSON

Writers build stories on conflict, the struggles a character faces. An external conflict is a struggle with something outside the character: another person, a group, or the world itself. Daniel's external conflict is with Rome. An internal conflict is a struggle inside the character's own mind, between what they feel and what they are starting to question. By the end of these chapters, Daniel has both. In Chapter 5, at Rabbi Hezron's home, Daniel hears the patient case that the Law, not the sword, is Israel's real strength, and his hotheaded reply gets him sent out. Then in Chapter 8, near Bethsaida, Daniel watches a teacher named Jesus heal a boy's crippled hand and many others, and speak of repenting and believing. Daniel had hoped Jesus would be a warrior who would lead a revolt. Instead the words confuse him. That confusion is the start of an internal conflict: the hatred he has chosen begins, very slightly, to be questioned. As you read, separate the two fights. One is against Rome. The other is inside Daniel.

Conflict typeWhat it isIn Daniel
Externala struggle with someone or something outside the characterDaniel against Rome and its soldiers
Internala struggle inside the character's own mindDaniel's hatred beginning to be questioned by what Jesus says
💡 WHO IS TELLING YOU ALL THIS?

Three voices argue in these chapters. Now notice a fourth thing: somebody is deciding which of them you get to hear, and how. That somebody is the narrator, and the position the narrator takes is called the point of view. Speare uses a limited point of view. You are inside Daniel's head and nobody else's. You know what Daniel thinks about Rosh; you never once find out what Rosh thinks about Daniel. The other choice a writer has is an omniscient point of view, a word that means all-knowing, where the narrator can move into anyone's thoughts at will. Speare could have written this book that way and chose not to, and the choice does real work. Because Rosh only ever reaches you through Daniel's eyes, you inherit Daniel's admiration in the early chapters without questioning it. Later, when Daniel finally sees "a man he had never really looked at before," you are not being told that Rosh was hollow. You are finding it out at the moment Daniel does. A narrator who knew everything would have spoiled that. Ask this of every book from now on: who is telling me this, and what are they not able to see?

Point of viewWhat the narrator knowsWhat it does to you
Limitedone character's thoughts onlyYou are trapped with that character's blind spots, and you discover things when they do
Omniscienteveryone's thoughtsYou see more than any character does, and you may know a mistake is coming before they make it
💡 TRY THIS
Add point of view, limited, and omniscient to your Word Bank. Then take the dinner argument in Chapter 5 and write three or four sentences of it from Hezron's point of view instead. You will have to invent what Hezron thinks, because the book never tells you. That is exactly the information a limited point of view keeps from you.

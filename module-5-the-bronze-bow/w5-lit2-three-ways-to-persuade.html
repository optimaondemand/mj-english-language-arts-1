Three Ways to Persuade

📚 THE LESSON

There are three main ways to persuade, and naming them makes your essay sharper. Logos is an appeal to reason: facts, logic, and evidence that lead a listener to a conclusion. Ethos is an appeal to character: people believe you because of who you are, your reputation, your honesty, or your experience. Pathos is an appeal to emotion: you make a listener feel something, pity, fear, or hope, so strongly that they act. Speare fills The Bronze Bow with all three. When Hezron lays out the patient case against the Zealots, warning that resistance will bring "rows of crosses, and burning villages, and heavier taxes," that is logos: a reasoned argument from cause and effect. When Rosh wins followers because he is the fearless, undefeated leader, that is ethos: people follow his reputation. And when Daniel tells Joel the story of his crucified father to win the oath, that is pathos: an appeal straight to the heart. The same three tools are the ones you will use in your own essay. You will reason from the evidence (logos), write as a fair and careful reader the audience can trust (ethos), and let the real weight of Daniel's story carry feeling where it belongs (pathos). Today, learn the three by name, find each one in the book, then write a working thesis about what Daniel's journey means.

LOGOS · an appeal to reason
Facts and logic that lead to a conclusion. Click to see who uses it.
Hezron (Chapter 5). He argues from cause and effect that fighting Rome will bring only "rows of crosses, and burning villages, and heavier taxes." Rosh also uses cold logos in Chapter 9: a fight "will take men and arms and food. And they have to be paid for."
ETHOS · an appeal to character
Belief based on who the speaker is. Click to see who uses it.
Rosh (Chapter 2). Men follow him because of his reputation as the fearless, undefeated leader. The book later takes that reputation apart, when Daniel finally sees "a man he had never really looked at before" (Chapter 18). Simon's quiet, lived credibility is a truer ethos.
PATHOS · an appeal to emotion
Persuasion through feeling. Click to see who uses it.
Daniel (Chapter 7). He tells Joel the story of his crucified father and uncle, and the grief and anger of that story win the oath. The appeal goes straight to the heart, not the head.
⚠️ WHEN AN APPEAL GOES WRONG

Logos, ethos, and pathos are tools, and tools are not good or bad by themselves. A speaker uses them badly when an appeal replaces a reason instead of carrying one. That failure has a name. It is a fallacy, a mistake in reasoning, and you have met three already: the straw man in Module 2, the either/or in Module 3, and the hasty generalization in Module 4. Here is the difference that matters. Ethos becomes a fallacy when a speaker expects you to believe something only because of who is saying it. Watch what Speare does with Rosh. Men join him because he is the bold, undefeated leader, and for a long time that reputation stands in for any reason at all. Then Daniel finally sees "a man he had never really looked at before," and the reputation turns out to have been carrying weight it could not hold. Pathos becomes a fallacy the same way, when feeling arrives instead of a reason rather than alongside one. Reread the passageway oath and ask an honest question: does Daniel give Joel reasons to join, or does he give him grief? A third failure is the ad hominem, which means attacking the person instead of answering what they said. If someone replies "you are too young to understand" rather than answering your point, they have not answered you at all. None of this makes the appeals off limits. Your essay will use all three. It means you owe your reader a reason underneath the feeling.

📊 THE SAME TOOL, USED TWO WAYS
AN APPEAL (FAIR)
"Simon has cared for the sick in this village for years, so his judgment about Daniel is worth weighing." Reputation supports a reason.
A FALLACY (UNFAIR)
"Rosh is undefeated, so he must be right." Reputation replaces the reason entirely.

Lesson 5.28
Parallel Structure, Colons, and Semicolons
Grammar & Vocabulary · Week 5
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Matched parts and the right mark make a sentence land.
You'll learn to keep items in a list in the same form (parallel structure) and to use a colon and a semicolon correctly. These are the moves that make an essay sentence sound finished. Then a short quiz checks what you've got. You'll use all three in your essay this week.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to fix a list so its parts are parallel (in the same form).
2
I will be able to use a colon to introduce and a semicolon to join.
⚡ WARM-UP
Quick ear test. Which sounds smoother: "Daniel vowed to hate, to fight, and to kill" or "Daniel vowed to hate, fighting, and that he would kill"? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THE LESSON

Parallel structure means the items in a list are all in the same grammatical form. "Daniel vowed to hate them, to fight them, and to kill them" is parallel, because each part starts with "to" plus a verb. "Daniel vowed to hate them, fighting them, and that he would kill them" is not, because the forms do not match, and the sentence stumbles. When you list two or more things, keep them the same shape. A colon introduces what follows: a list, an example, or an explanation. The rule is that a full sentence must come before the colon. "Daniel faced three voices: Rosh, Hezron, and Jesus" is correct, because "Daniel faced three voices" could stand alone. A semicolon joins two complete sentences that are closely related, where you feel a period is too strong. "Rosh chose the sword; Jesus chose love" uses a semicolon to set the two halves side by side. Use a colon to point forward to something, and a semicolon to link two sentences that belong together. As you write your essay this week, parallel lists and the right mark will make your reasoning sound controlled and clear.

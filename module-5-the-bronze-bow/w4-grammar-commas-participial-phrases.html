Lesson 5.22
Commas and Participial Phrases: Showing Two Things at Once
Grammar & Vocabulary · Week 4
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
One sentence can show a person acting and feeling at the same time.
You'll learn the participial phrase, a phrase built on an -ing or -ed verb form that describes a noun, and the comma that sets it off when it opens a sentence. This is the tool that lets you write "Crossing the road, Daniel asked Marcus inside" instead of two flat sentences.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify a participial phrase and the noun it describes.
2
I will be able to place the comma correctly after an opening participial phrase and avoid a dangling one.
⚡ WARM-UP
Quick gut check: in "Trembling with fear, Leah reached for Daniel's hand," which words describe Leah? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THE LESSON

A participle is a verb form used to describe a noun. It usually ends in -ing (running, trembling) or -ed (broken, healed). A participial phrase is a participle plus the words attached to it: trembling with fear, healed by Jesus, crossing the road. The phrase lets you show an action and a description at once, which is why writers reach for it in important moments. Two rules keep it clean. First, when a participial phrase opens a sentence, put a comma after it: "Crossing the road, Daniel asked Marcus inside." Second, the phrase must describe the noun right after the comma, or it dangles and confuses the reader. "Healed by Jesus, Leah smiled" is correct, because Leah is the one healed. "Healed by Jesus, the room felt lighter" is wrong, because a room cannot be healed. As you write your essay about Daniel's change, the participial phrase is how you pack feeling and action into one strong sentence. Aim it at the right noun, and set it off with a comma.

📚 THE OTHER TWO VERBALS, PUT TO WORK

Back in Module 1 you learned that a participle has two relatives. All three are verbals, verb forms doing some other job in a sentence. Now that participial phrases have returned, take the other two out and use them, because they do something your essay needs. A gerund is an -ing form acting as a noun, which means it can be the subject of a sentence, and that lets you make an action the thing you are talking about. "Forgiving cost Daniel more than fighting ever had" puts two actions head to head as though they were objects you could weigh. An infinitive is to plus a verb, and it is how English talks about choice and purpose: "Daniel chose to stay." Notice how naturally that fits a book about a boy deciding what to do with his hatred. The old confusion is still the only hard part: gerunds and participles both end in -ing, so do not look at the ending, look at the job. Describing somebody means participle. Naming the thing itself means gerund.

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

When you describe the ending in your essay, try an opening participial phrase. "Crossing the road, Daniel opened his door to the enemy" shows the action and the meaning in one breath. Just make sure the phrase points at the right person.

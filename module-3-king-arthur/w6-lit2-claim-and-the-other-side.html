Your Claim, and the Other Side of It
Literature · Week 6 · Project Week
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The strongest argument is the one that has already heard the other side.
Today you turn your chosen difference into a claim: one debatable sentence about what makes a good knight. Then you do the move that lifts a comparison into a real argument: you state, fairly and honestly, the strongest case for the other knight, and answer it. A claim that admits no other side is weak. A claim that answers the best objection is strong.

📚 THE LESSON

A claim is a sentence someone could reasonably disagree with. "Gareth was a knight" is not a claim; it is a fact. "Green suggests Gareth is a better knight than Launcelot, because honor earned by service is worth more than honor born from rank" is a claim, because a careful reader could argue the other way. Once you have your claim, do the move most essays skip. Find the strongest case for the other knight and state it honestly, not as a weak version you can knock down easily. If you argue for Gareth over Launcelot, you must admit that Launcelot is the greatest knight in arms, generous and gentle, the one who could face dangers no other knight could. Only after you have stated that fairly do you answer it: yes, but Green shows that skill in battle is not the same as goodness, and Launcelot's divided heart breaks the very fellowship he was sworn to protect. Naming the opposing reading and answering it is what makes your essay an argument instead of a list. Today, write your claim, then write the best objection to it, then answer the objection. That three-part shape is the spine of your essay.

💡 TRY THIS IN YOUR READING JOURNAL · WORD WORK
Write your objection sentence using a fair-minded opener like "Of course," "It is true that," or "Some readers might argue that." Then write the answer with a turn word like "yet" or "however." Add one line: does the objection feel honest, or did you make it weak on purpose?
📊 THE SPINE OF YOUR ARGUMENT
1. YOUR CLAIM
A debatable sentence: which knight better fits Green's idea of a good one, and why.
2. THE BEST OBJECTION
The strongest, fairest case for the other knight, stated honestly.
3. YOUR ANSWER
Why, even granting that, your knight better fits what Green calls good.
Write all three in your journal before you touch your draft.
⚠️ A SECOND FALLACY: THE EITHER/OR

In Module 2 you met the straw man, the fake version of the other side built to be easy to beat. Here is a second fallacy, and this one may be hiding inside your own essay question. "Which knight is better, Gareth or Launcelot?" quietly invites you to assume there are only two possibilities and that one of them must win completely. That assumption has a name. It is called an either/or, and it works by offering two choices as though no others existed. "A knight is either brave or courteous." "Either you follow the law or you do what is right." Read those again and you can feel the trick. Nothing stops a knight from being both brave and courteous, and Green gives you knights who are. Spotting an either/or does not mean abandoning your claim. It means making sure your claim rests on a real difference in the text rather than on a forced choice. When you write your objection today, check the choice you are handing your reader. Are those truly the only two options, or did you close a door the book left open?

💡 LISTEN FOR THESE WORDS
either … or · you have to pick one · there are only two kinds of people · if you are not X, you must be Y
None of these is automatically wrong. Sometimes there really are only two options. The test is whether a third one exists and is being hidden.
💡 TRY THIS IN YOUR READING JOURNAL
Add either/or to your Word Bank next to straw man. Then read your own claim aloud. If it forces a reader to choose between two things that could both be true, rewrite it so the difference you are arguing for is one the text actually shows.

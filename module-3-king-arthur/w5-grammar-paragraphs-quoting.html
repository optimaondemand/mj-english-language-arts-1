Lesson 3.28
Build a Body Paragraph That Proves Its Point
Grammar & Vocabulary · Week 5
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A quote does not prove your point on its own. Your explanation does.
A body paragraph is a small argument: a point, the proof, and your explanation. Today you learn the three-part shape (topic sentence, evidence, elaboration) and how to drop a quotation in cleanly and punctuate it correctly. Then a short quiz checks it. This is the exact skill your essay needs this week.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name the three parts of a body paragraph: topic sentence, evidence, elaboration.
2
I will be able to introduce a quotation with a comma and place the closing punctuation correctly.
⚡ WARM-UP
You tell a friend "Gareth is humble." They say "Prove it." What do you reach for first? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THE LESSON

A strong body paragraph has three moves. First the topic sentence states the one point the paragraph will prove (for example, "Gareth proves his worth through humble service, not rank"). Next comes the evidence, a specific moment or quotation from the book. Last comes the elaboration, your own sentences explaining how that evidence proves the point. The mistake most writers make is dropping a quote in and walking away, as if the quote argues for itself. It does not. You argue; the quote is your proof. Quoting cleanly has two simple rules at this level. When you introduce a quotation with a phrase like Green writes, put a comma before the opening quotation mark: Green writes, "Beaumains served always gentle and patient." And the closing punctuation usually goes inside the final quotation mark. Keep your borrowed words short, and let your own explanation carry the weight. When you write this week, build every body paragraph as point, proof, explanation, and your argument will hold.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL · A MOVE TO KEEP
elaboration (tap to see what it means)
Elaboration is the sentence or two after your evidence where you explain how it proves your point. Test it: if you deleted your quote, would your explanation still tell the reader what you wanted them to see? If not, add more elaboration.
📊 ONE BODY PARAGRAPH, LABELED
TOPIC SENTENCE: Gareth shows that a good knight earns honor through service, not through rank.
EVIDENCE: Though he is Arthur's own nephew, Gareth asks only for food and drink and serves a year in the kitchen under Sir Kay's mockery.
ELABORATION: By hiding his noble birth, Gareth makes the reader judge him by his deeds. Green is arguing that real worth is something a knight proves, not something he is simply born with.
📖 FROM THE BOOK
Pick one knight you are writing about. In your Reading Journal, draft one body paragraph in three labeled parts: topic sentence, one short quotation introduced with a comma, and two sentences of elaboration. (Use your own copy of the book to find and key the quotation accurately.)

1. Which is a strong topic sentence for a body paragraph?
2. Which sentence introduces and punctuates the quotation correctly?
3. After your quotation, which sentence is real elaboration?

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

From now on, never let a quotation stand alone. Point, proof, then explain. The explanation is where your thinking shows, and it is what makes a paragraph an argument instead of a summary.

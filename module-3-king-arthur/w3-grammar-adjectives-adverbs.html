Lesson 3.16
Adjectives Describe; Adverbs Tell How
Grammar & Vocabulary · Week 3
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
An adjective describes a thing. An adverb describes the action.
You'll sort adjectives from adverbs, learn the question each one answers, and practice the harder skill: choosing the precise modifier instead of a vague one. Gareth's story is full of both.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to tell an adjective (describes a noun) from an adverb (describes a verb, adjective, or adverb).
2
I will be able to choose a precise adverb that shows how an action is done.
⚡ WARM-UP
Quick gut check: which sentence shows you more? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THE LESSON

An adjective describes a noun and answers which one? what kind? how many? A patient youth, a scornful damsel, three enemies. An adverb describes a verb, an adjective, or another adverb, and most often answers how? when? where? how much? Gareth serves humbly; he answers Kay's jeers gently; the road grows steadily harder. A quick test: many adverbs end in -ly, but the surer test is the job the word does. If it describes a thing, it is an adjective; if it describes an action or another describer, it is an adverb. The skill that matters is the same one you practiced with adjectives: pick the precise word. "Gareth fought well" tells you little. "Gareth fought tirelessly" shows you a knight who would not quit. As you read, watch the adverbs Green attaches to Gareth's actions. Each one is a small argument about the kind of knight he is.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL · THE QUICK TEST
Adjective or adverb? Tap for the one-question test.
Ask: what does this word describe? If it describes a noun (a person, place, thing), it's an adjective. If it describes a verb, an adjective, or another adverb (usually answering how, when, where, or how much), it's an adverb. Do not trust the -ly ending alone: "friendly" and "lonely" are adjectives.
📚 WORDS THAT COMPARE · YOU WILL NEED THESE FOR YOUR ESSAY

The question asks which knight is better. That word is doing something specific, and it has a name. Adjectives and adverbs change form when they compare, and the three forms are called degrees. The plain form is what you use for one thing: brave. The comparative compares exactly two: braver. The superlative picks one out of three or more: bravest. Short words take -er and -est. Longer words use more and most instead, which is why you say more courteous and never courteouser. The mistake to avoid is using both at once. More braver is wrong, and so is most bravest; pick one method. A few common words ignore the rules entirely and simply have to be known: good, better, best and bad, worse, worst. Watch your own essay for this. If you are weighing two knights, the comparative is your word. Calling Gareth the bravest knight is a claim about every knight in the book, and you would have to prove it.

💡 TRY THIS
Write two sentences comparing Gareth and one other knight, using a comparative each time. Then write one superlative sentence and ask yourself honestly: can I prove that about every knight in the book? If not, change it to a comparative. That single swap will make your essay easier to defend.
⚡ COMMIT FIRST, THEN COMPARE
Decide: is this sentence correct, or does it need a fix? “Gareth fought more braver than the other squires.” Type your answer (a fix, or “already correct”) below.
Reveal the rule
It needs a fix. “More braver” doubles up two comparative methods at once (more and -er), and English only ever uses one. The correct sentence is “Gareth fought braver than the other squires.” Watch for the same trap with superlatives: “most bravest” should just be “bravest.”

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Adjectives and adverbs are the words that turn a plain sentence into a clear picture. When you draft your essay, reach for the precise one. "He fought tirelessly" beats "he fought a lot" every time.

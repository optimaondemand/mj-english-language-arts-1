Lesson 3.22
Joining Ideas: Simple, Compound, Complex
Grammar & Vocabulary · Week 4
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Conjunctions are the small words that show how two ideas connect.
You'll learn three sentence types (simple, compound, complex) and the conjunctions that build them. This is the most useful sentence skill for your essay, where you often need to join two ideas: Launcelot is the best knight, but he cannot achieve the Grail. Then a short quiz checks what you've got.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to tell a simple, compound, and complex sentence apart.
2
I will be able to use a conjunction to join two ideas so the connection between them is clear.
⚡ WARM-UP
Quick gut check: which sentence shows the connection between the two ideas more clearly? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THE LESSON

A clause is a group of words with its own subject and verb. A simple sentence is one clause that stands on its own: "Galahad took the seat." A compound sentence joins two complete clauses with a comma and a coordinating conjunction (and, but, or, so, for, nor, yet): "Galahad was pure, so he achieved the Grail." A complex sentence joins a complete clause to one that cannot stand alone, using a subordinating conjunction like because, although, when, since, while: "Because Launcelot's heart was divided, he saw the Grail only in part." The reason this matters for your essay is simple: an argument is full of ideas that connect, and the conjunction names the connection. Use but or although to show tension, because or so to show cause. As you write, mix the three types. A page of nothing but simple sentences sounds choppy; a few well-joined ones make your reasoning clear.

📊 THREE SENTENCE TYPES
TypeHow it's builtExample
Simpleone complete clause"Galahad took the seat."
Compoundtwo clauses + comma + and/but/or/so"Galahad was pure, so he achieved the Grail."
Complexone clause + because/although/when + a clause that can't stand alone"Because his heart was divided, Launcelot saw the Grail only in part."
⚡ TAP TO REVEAL · A QUICK TEST
Coordinating or subordinating? Tap for the test.
A coordinating conjunction (and, but, or, so) joins two ideas of equal weight, so either half could stand alone. A subordinating conjunction (because, although, when, since) makes one idea depend on the other, so the half it starts cannot stand alone ("Because his heart was divided" is not a full sentence by itself).
📚 A CONJUNCTION IN DISGUISE: THE ADVERB THAT JOINS

Back in Module 2 you practiced moving between ideas with words like therefore and as a result. You have been using these for months. Now you get the name for them, and the punctuation rule that trips most writers up. A conjunctive adverb (however, therefore, moreover, nevertheless, instead) connects two complete ideas the way and or but does, but it needs different punctuation. Put a semicolon before it, since it is joining two complete sentences, and a comma right after it, since it is easing you into the second one: "Launcelot was the greatest knight in the hall; however, his heart was divided." Word choice matters too: however and nevertheless signal contrast, the way but does, while therefore and moreover build on what came before, the way so and also do. The mistake to avoid is treating however like a comma word between two full ideas. "Launcelot was skilled, however his heart was divided" is a common error; a comma alone is too weak to hold two complete ideas together. If both sides could stand as their own sentence, you need the semicolon.

📚 A THIRD KIND: CONJUNCTIONS THAT WORK IN PAIRS

There is one more kind, and it never travels alone. A correlative conjunction comes in a matched pair, with one word in front of each thing being joined: either … or, neither … nor, both … and, not only … but also, whether … or. They are useful in an argument because they hold two things up side by side, which is exactly what your essay does with two knights. There is one rule, and it is about balance: whatever follows the first half should match the form of whatever follows the second. "Gareth is not only brave but also courteous" works because brave and courteous are both adjectives. "Gareth is not only brave but also he treats the damsel well" wobbles, because an adjective is being matched with a whole clause. Read the sentence aloud; the wobble is usually audible before you can explain it.

⚠️ ONE THING TO WATCH, AND IT IS NOT GRAMMAR
You will meet either … or again in Week 6, where you will learn that offering a reader only two choices can be a trap in an argument when a third choice really exists. The grammar here is perfectly correct. It is the thinking behind it that has to be checked. A sentence can be well built and still say something unfair.

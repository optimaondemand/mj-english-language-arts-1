Lesson 3.07
The Green Knight's Game: Building Suspense
Literature · Week 2
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A good story makes you wait, and the waiting is the point.
A giant knight, green from head to foot, rides into Arthur's hall and offers a deadly game: strike me once, and in a year and a day I strike you back. Watch how the writer stretches the danger across a whole year. The craft term for the tension that makes you want to keep reading is suspense.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain what suspense is and how the year-and-a-day promise creates it.
2
I will be able to point to one detail that shows why Gawain takes the Green Knight's challenge.

🏛️ A LITTLE BACKGROUND FIRST
This tale comes from an old medieval poem that Green retells. The setting is a Christmas (Yule) feast at Camelot, the season's high celebration. In these legends, a knight's word is sacred: a promise made in public must be kept, whatever the cost. The Green Knight arrives during the feast and offers a "Yule-tide sport," a deadly bargain disguised as a game. Hold onto that idea of the binding promise. It is the engine of the whole chapter.
📚 THE LESSON

Suspense is the tension a writer builds to keep you wondering what will happen. The Green Knight's offer is built for it: one knight may strike him with the axe now, but must take an equal blow in return a year and a day later. When Gawain beheads him in a single stroke, the story seems over, until the Green Knight calmly picks up his own severed head, which speaks, names the meeting place, and rides out. Now the danger is delayed, not ended, and that delay is the suspense. Every reader knows Gawain owes a blow he cannot dodge. Notice why Gawain takes the challenge in the first place: he steps forward to protect Arthur and to honor a guest's strange request, which tells you something about his courtesy and his courage. Your job today is to notice how the writer stretches the danger over time and what Gawain's choice reveals about him.

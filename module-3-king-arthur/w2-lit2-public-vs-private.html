The Real Test: Honor When No One Is Watching
Literature · Week 2
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The hardest fight in a story is sometimes inside one person.
Gawain's real test is not the axe. It is three quiet temptations and one small lie about a magic green girdle. A struggle between a character and an outside force is external conflict; a struggle inside a character's own heart is internal conflict. Today you watch both, and see why one small lie costs Gawain a nick on the neck.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to tell internal conflict from external conflict in Gawain's story.
2
I will be able to explain why the nick on Gawain's neck is a fair result of his one hidden lie.

📚 THE LESSON

Conflict is the struggle that drives a story. External conflict is a character against an outside force: Gawain against the Green Knight and his deadly bargain. Internal conflict is a struggle inside a character: Gawain's fear of death pulling against his promise to be honest. On the road Gawain reaches a castle whose lord makes a friendly bargain: each day they will trade whatever each has won. For two days Gawain courteously refuses the lord's wife and honestly hands over what he received. On the third day she gives him a green girdle she says will keep its wearer from being slain, and Gawain, afraid of the blow he owes, accepts it and hides it from his host. That single concealed lie is his one fault. At the Green Chapel the Green Knight, revealed as the castle's lord, feints twice and gives only a small nick on the third blow, punishment for the lie, mercy for everything Gawain did right. Your job today is to separate the outside danger from the struggle inside Gawain.

💡 WHY YOU KNOW WHAT THE CASTLE DOES NOT

Stop and notice something easy to miss. You know Gawain kept the girdle. The lord's household does not. Nobody at that castle sees him take it or hide it, yet you watched him do both. That is not an accident of the story. It is a choice about point of view, which means the position the narrator takes and therefore what you are allowed to see. This narrator can step past a closed door and show you a private act, so you carry a piece of knowledge through the next two days that every character at the table lacks. It is why the third morning is uncomfortable to read. Gawain is courteous in public and carrying a lie in private, and you are the only one holding both halves. Watch for this move everywhere: a story feels tense not only because of what happens, but because of the gap the narrator opens between what a character shows and what you have been shown.

📊 TWO GAWAINS, ONE SCENE
▶ What the castle sees: a courteous guest who honours the bargain.
▶ What you were shown: a frightened man who kept the one thing he thought would save him.
💡 TRY THIS IN YOUR READING JOURNAL
Add point of view to your Word Bank. Then answer in two or three sentences: if the story never let you behind that door, and you only saw what the household saw, how would the Green Chapel scene change for you? Would the nick on Gawain's neck still make sense?
💡 NAME THIS NARRATOR'S REACH

You already noticed that this narrator can show you something no character in the castle sees. That is the mark of an omniscient narrator, one who is not stuck inside a single character's head and can reveal private moments, hidden identities, or scenes far apart. Later in this same tale you learn that the castle's lord is secretly the Green Knight himself, something Gawain does not learn until the very end. A limited narrator, the kind St. George and the Dragon uses, could never do this: it would stay locked to what Marcellus or George alone can see and know. This tale's narrator ranges further. It does not switch between several characters' own points of view, that would be a different technique, it simply knows more than any one character does.

⚡ PLACE IT · LIMITED OR OMNISCIENT?
For each moment, decide how close this narrator is to all-knowing (omniscient) or locked to one character (limited).
1. The narrator shows you Gawain hiding the girdle, something no character at the castle witnesses.
2. Later, the story reveals that the castle's lord is secretly the Green Knight, a fact Gawain did not know while he was there.
3. In St. George and the Dragon, the narrator never once tells you Regina's or the dragon's private thoughts directly.

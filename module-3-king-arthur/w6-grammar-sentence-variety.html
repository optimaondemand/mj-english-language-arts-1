Lesson 3.34
Vary Your Sentences So the Reader Stays Awake
Grammar & Vocabulary · Week 6
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
When every sentence sounds the same, the reader stops hearing them.
Your essay is almost done. Today you make the prose itself stronger by varying your sentence length and openings. You'll learn to combine choppy short sentences and to start a sentence somewhere other than the subject.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to combine two short, choppy sentences into one smoother sentence.
2
I will be able to open a sentence with something other than the subject for variety.
⚡ WARM-UP
Read these aloud in your head: "Gareth was brave. Gareth was humble. Gareth served Kay." How do they sound? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THE LESSON

Sentence variety means mixing your sentence lengths and your sentence openings on purpose, so the writing has rhythm. Two tools do most of the work. The first is combining. When you have short, choppy sentences that repeat ("Gareth was brave. Gareth was humble."), join them: "Gareth was both brave and humble." You can also use a connecting word like and, but, or because to show how two ideas relate ("Gareth was noble by birth, but he chose to serve in the kitchen."). The second tool is varying the opening. Most sentences start with the subject ("Gareth served Kay for a year."). For variety, sometimes start somewhere else: with a time word ("For a year, Gareth served Kay."), or with a describing phrase ("Mocked daily, Gareth never answered back."). Notice the goal is not long sentences everywhere. A short sentence after several long ones can land hard, on purpose. The point is to choose, not to fall into the same shape every time. As you do your final polish, read your essay aloud in your head and listen for runs of identical sentences. Combine some, reshape the openings of others, and your argument will sound as sharp as it is.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL · A MOVE TO KEEP
sentence opening (tap to see what it means)
A sentence opening is the first word or phrase before you reach the subject. Test your writing: if five sentences in a row all open with the subject (a name or "He" / "The"), reshape one or two to open with a time word or a describing phrase instead.
📊 CHOPPY, THEN VARIED
CHOPPY: Gareth was a prince. Gareth hid his rank. Gareth worked in the kitchen. Gareth was mocked. Gareth stayed patient.
VARIED: Gareth was a prince, but he hid his rank and worked in the kitchen. Mocked daily, he stayed patient.
Five short, repetitive sentences become two: one combined, one with a varied opening.
📖 FROM YOUR ESSAY
Open your essay draft and find one place where two or three short sentences repeat the same opening. In your Reading Journal, rewrite them as one combined sentence and one with a varied opening. (This is real polish for your final draft.)
⚡ TRY IT · COMBINE AND RESHAPE
Choose the strongest revision for each. Pick one and you'll get instant feedback.
1. Combine: "Launcelot was the best knight. Launcelot could not achieve the Grail."
2. Reshape the opening: "Gareth served in the kitchen for a year."
3. After three long sentences about the fall, you write: "The realm of Logres was broken." This short sentence is…
💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Read your essay aloud in your head one more time. Where the sentences thud, combine or reshape. A varied rhythm is the last thing that turns a finished draft into a polished one.

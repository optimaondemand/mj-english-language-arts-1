Then Lancelot standing near, “Sir Seneschal, Sleuth-hound thou knowest, and gray, and all the hounds; A horse thou knowest, a man thou dost not know: Broad brows and fair, a fluent hair and fine, High nose, a nostril large and fine, and hands Large, fair and fine!—Some young lad’s mystery— But, or from sheepcot or king’s hall, the boy Is noble-natured. Treat him with all grace, Lest he should come to shame thy judging of him.” Then Kay, “What murmurest thou of mystery? Think ye this fellow will poison the King’s dish? Nay, for he spake too fool-like: mystery! Tut, an the lad were noble, he had asked For horse and armour: fair and fine, forsooth! Sir Fine-face, Sir Fair-hands? but see thou to it That thine own fineness, Lancelot, some fine day Undo thee not—and leave my man to me.”
Seneschal: the steward, the officer who runs a great household (here Sir Kay) · sleuth-hound: a hound that tracks by scent · fluent: flowing · sheepcot: a sheep pen · noble-natured: noble in character · lest: in case · murmurest: mutter · spake: spoke · an: if · forsooth: indeed, said mockingly · undo: ruin
Tennyson spells the names Lancelot, Lynette, and Lyonors. Our book says Launcelot, Linnet, and Liones.

✏️ Grammar: Learn
Step 1 of 3 this week: adjectives, adverbs, and words that compare.

An adjective describes a noun. An adverb describes a verb, and sometimes an adjective or another adverb. Some words compare: one method per word, never two.

WordWhat it doesExample
adjectivedescribes a noun: which one? what kind? how many?tall stranger, white palfrey
adverbdescribes a verb: how? when? where? how much?answered politely
the -ly trapmany adverbs end in -ly, but some -ly words describe nounsa lowly office, a friendly smile
comparativecompares two things: -er or moregentler, more courteous
superlativecompares three or more things: -est or mostgentlest, most courteous
never bothone method at a timenot more gentler, not most bravest
no rulea few words change completelygood, better, best

📌 Watch how
Theo’s paragraph
6th Grade Scholar

A good knight keeps his word even when it costs him. Sir Gawain and Gareth both make hard promises, but Gareth keeps his more completely. Gawain rides out to meet the Green Knight, just as he swore he would. Yet he also swore to hand over everything he won in the castle, and he secretly keeps the green lace. Gareth promises King Arthur to finish Lady Linnet’s adventure. She mocks him at every step, but he answers politely, “you may say to me what you wish, but never will I turn back.” He stays at her side until the quest is done. Gawain is the more famous knight, but Gareth is the more faithful one, because his promise never bends.

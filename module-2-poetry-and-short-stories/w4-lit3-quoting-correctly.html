Use Their Words Fairly: Quoting and Paraphrasing
Literature · Week 4
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
When you use an author's words, use them exactly and mark them clearly.
Evidence is only fair if you handle the words honestly. Today you learn to introduce a quotation the right way and to paraphrase without changing the meaning. Then you add a correct quote and a correct paraphrase to your letter.

📚 THE LESSON

There are two honest ways to use an author's words. The first is a quotation, where you copy the exact words and mark them clearly. The standard shape is a signal phrase (a few words naming who is speaking), then a comma, then the exact words inside quotation marks: Hawthorne writes, "Gold is not everything." The signal phrase tells the reader whose words follow, the comma sets them off, and the quotation marks promise that everything between them is exact. Change even one word and you may no longer use quotation marks. The second way is a paraphrase, where you put the author's idea into your own words. A paraphrase takes no quotation marks because the words are now yours, but it must keep the author's meaning. You cannot make Hawthorne say "money means nothing at all" when he wrote "Gold is not everything." That twists his point. Either way, name the author so the reader knows the idea is theirs, not yours. Quote when the exact words matter. Paraphrase when you only need the idea. Used fairly, both give your letter the proof it needs without distorting the author you are answering.

💡 TRY THIS IN YOUR READING JOURNAL · WORD WORK
To paraphrase is to restate an idea in your own words. Start with this sentence: "I will paraphrase the author's main point in one sentence." Rewrite it three ways: swap a synonym for paraphrase (such as restate or put in my own words), stretch it by naming the point you mean, or keep its shape but write about paraphrasing something a friend said. Add one line: when is a quote better than a paraphrase?
⚡ TAP TO REVEAL · THE QUOTATION SHAPE
How do I introduce a quotation? (tap to see)
Signal phrase + comma + "exact words." Example: Hawthorne writes, "Gold is not everything." The period goes inside the closing quotation mark. Copy the words exactly; if you change them, drop the quotation marks and call it a paraphrase instead.

Lesson 2.13
The Turning Point: When a Gift Becomes a Curse
Literature · Week 3
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Every story has one moment where everything changes. Today you find it.
Yesterday you read the whole plot. Today you study the turning point, the single moment when Midas's lucky gift turns into the worst thing that ever happened to him. You will also learn a word for what Hawthorne is doing all along: irony.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name the turning point of "The Golden Touch" and explain why it is the turning point.
2
I will be able to explain irony: a gap between what is expected and what really happens.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
"The Golden Touch" by Nathaniel Hawthorne. (The full story is in the drop-down on yesterday's lesson page; reread the breakfast and Marygold scenes.)
⚡ WARM-UP · CHECK IN FIRST
Reading yesterday, when did you first feel sure the wish was a mistake? (No wrong answer. Just check in.)
📚 THE LESSON

A turning point is the moment in a story when things change direction and cannot go back to how they were. Up to that moment the trouble is still small and fixable. After it, everything is different. In "The Golden Touch" the turning point is sharp and clear: Midas bends down to comfort his crying daughter, kisses her, and she hardens into a golden statue with "yellow tear-drops congealing on her cheeks." Before that kiss, his problem was an annoying one. He could not eat his breakfast. After it, his problem is the worst one a person can have. He has destroyed the person he loves most. That moment also shows you irony, which is a gap between what someone expects and what actually happens. The whole story runs on it. Midas asks for a gift that will make him perfectly happy, and the gift makes him perfectly miserable. He thinks gold is the most valuable thing in the world, and gold is exactly what takes his daughter away. Hawthorne uses one more kind of irony too. From early on, you can see the wish is dangerous, but Midas cannot. When the reader knows something a character does not, that is dramatic irony, and it is why you spend the breakfast scene wanting to shout a warning he will never hear.

💡 TRY THIS · WORD WORK
Reread the moment Midas kisses Marygold. Find the sentence that calls her "the victim of his insatiable desire for wealth." Insatiable means impossible to satisfy, never having enough. Copy that sentence, then rewrite it one of three ways: swap in a word that means almost the same as insatiable (a synonym), stretch the sentence with more detail, or keep its shape but write about a different want that can never be filled. Add one line: why is "insatiable" the exact right word for Midas?
📊 THE HINGE OF THE STORY
BEFORE THE KISS
The losses are small and fixable: a book he cannot read, useless spectacles, a breakfast he cannot eat. Annoying, not tragic.
➡️
AFTER THE KISS
Marygold is a golden statue. The loss is total and cannot be undone by him. Everything in the story now bends toward fixing it.
The kiss is the hinge. A turning point is the door the story swings through and cannot swing back.
📖 REREAD CLOSELY
Go back to the full story in the drop-down on Lesson 2.12. Reread two scenes: the breakfast (where the food turns to metal) and the moment Midas kisses Marygold. As you reread, underline in your notes one place where you, the reader, know more than Midas does. That is the dramatic irony at work.

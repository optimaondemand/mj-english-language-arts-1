Lesson 2.20
Words That Connect Your Ideas: Transitions
Grammar & Vocabulary · Week 4
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A transition is a signpost. It tells the reader what your next sentence is doing.
In an argument, the reader needs to know whether your next sentence adds to your point, pushes against it, or draws a conclusion. Transitions are the words that say which. You'll learn the main kinds, then use them in your letter.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name what a transition word is doing (adding, contrasting, or concluding).
2
I will be able to choose the right transition to connect a claim to its evidence.
⚡ WARM-UP
Which word signals that an opposite idea is coming next? (No wrong answer. We'll sort it out together.)
📚 THE LESSON

A transition is a word or phrase that shows how one idea connects to the next. Think of it as a road sign for the reader. In argument writing there are three jobs you will use most. Some transitions add, telling the reader you are stacking on another point of the same kind: also, in addition, furthermore, moreover. Some transitions contrast, warning the reader that an opposite or qualifying idea is coming: however, but, although, on the other hand, even so. These are the most important ones for your letter, because disagreeing with an author is one long contrast. And some transitions conclude, signaling that you are drawing a result or a final point: therefore, as a result, so, in the end. The trick is to match the word to the move. If your next sentence answers the author, reach for a contrast word: "You argue that wealth is dangerous. However, the story shows it was greed, not gold, that did the harm." If your next sentence draws the lesson, reach for a conclusion word: "Therefore, I think you are half right." Used well, transitions make your reasoning easy to follow, which is exactly what a strong letter needs.

ADD
also, in addition, furthermore, moreover
CONTRAST
however, but, although, on the other hand
CONCLUDE
therefore, as a result, so, in the end
📚 THESE ONES HAVE A NAME, AND A PUNCTUATION RULE

Look again at however, therefore, and as a result. Words like these have a name: they are conjunctive adverbs. The name is worth knowing because they come with a rule that trips up good writers constantly. A conjunctive adverb is not a conjunction like and or but. It cannot hold two complete sentences together with only a comma. When you join two complete sentences with one, the mark in front of it is a semicolon, and a comma goes after it. Get that wrong and you have written a run-on, which is two complete sentences joined with nothing strong enough to hold them. Say the sentence aloud and listen: if both halves could stand alone as sentences, you need the semicolon.

📊 THE SAME SENTENCE, RIGHT AND WRONG
RUN-ON
Midas got exactly what he asked for, however, he lost the one thing he loved.
CORRECT
Midas got exactly what he asked for; however, he lost the one thing he loved.
Also correct, and often better: split it into two sentences. Midas got exactly what he asked for. However, he lost the one thing he loved.
💡 THE COMMON ONES
however · therefore · moreover · consequently · nevertheless · instead · otherwise · meanwhile
📝 IN YOUR LETTER
Find the place where you turn from the author's view to your own answer, and add a contrast transition like however or although. Then find your closing and add a conclusion transition like therefore. Write both new sentences into your draft.
⚡ TRY IT · MATCH THE MOVE
Choose the best answer for each. Pick one and you'll get instant feedback.
1. You want to turn from the author's view to your own answer. Best transition?
2. Which transition signals a conclusion?
3. Which sentence uses an adding transition correctly?
💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Read your letter and underline every transition. If two sentences sit side by side with no signpost between them, ask what they are doing, then add the word that says so.

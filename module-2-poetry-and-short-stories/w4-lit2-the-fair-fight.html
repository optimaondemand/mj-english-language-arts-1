Lesson 2.18
The Fair Fight: State the Other Side, Then Answer It
Literature · Week 4
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The strongest way to disagree is to state the other side at its best, then answer it.
Today you learn the move that makes your letter convincing. You give the author's view its strongest, fairest form, then answer it with evidence from the text. A weak letter knocks down a view nobody holds. A strong one beats the best version.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to state the strongest, fairest version of a counter-argument before I answer it.
2
I will be able to answer that view with specific evidence from the text.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
Your project file, with the claim and counter-claim you wrote yesterday
▶
Your Reading Journal and your own copy or notes on the chosen text, for finding evidence
⚡ WARM-UP · CHECK IN FIRST
When someone argues with you, which feels harder to answer? (No wrong answer. Just think about it.)
📚 THE LESSON

A counter-argument is the opposing side, the case against your claim. There is a tempting, lazy way to handle it. You make the author sound foolish and knock down a weak version of their view. The problem is that beating a weak version proves nothing. Anyone watching can see you ducked the real point. The honest and far more convincing move is to state the other side at its strongest and fairest, the version the author would actually agree is theirs, and then answer that. So in your letter you write something like: "You would say, Mr. Hawthorne, that Midas only found peace once he stopped chasing gold, and that is a real and powerful point." Then you turn and answer it with evidence, which is specific proof from the text: an event, a detail, or a quoted line. You might answer: "But the story also shows that it was the river water, free to everyone, that healed Marygold. Wealth was never the villain. Greed was." Notice the rhythm. State the strong opposing point, then answer it with something specific the text actually says or shows. Vague answers ("I just disagree") do not count. Evidence is what turns an opinion into an argument.

💡 TRY THIS IN YOUR READING JOURNAL · WORD WORK
Strong arguments rest on evidence. Evidence means the specific facts or details that prove a point. Start with this sentence: "The evidence from the text supports my claim." Rewrite it three ways: swap a synonym for evidence (such as proof or support), stretch it by naming the exact moment you mean, or keep its shape but write about evidence for a claim in your own life. Add one line: what is the single best piece of evidence in your text?
📊 THE MOVE: STATE THE STRONG SIDE, THEN ANSWER IT
STEP 1: STATE IT FAIRLY
"You would say that Midas only found peace once he gave up gold, a fair and powerful point."
➡️
STEP 2: ANSWER WITH EVIDENCE
"But it was free river water, not poverty, that healed Marygold. Greed was the villain, not wealth itself."
⚠️ THE WEAK MOVE HAS A NAME

The lazy version you just read about is common enough that it has a name. It is called a straw man. The picture behind the name is a scarecrow: easy to knock over, and nothing like a real person. In an argument, a straw man is a fake version of the other side, built to be easy to beat. Say a classmate writes, "Custard is brave because he acts even though he is frightened." If you answer, "So you think being scared is the same as being brave?", you have not answered your classmate. You have answered someone who never spoke. A mistake in reasoning like this is called a fallacy, and the straw man is one of the most common. Once you know the name, you start seeing it everywhere: in arguments online, in advertisements, and in your own first drafts. The test is the one you already have. Would the other person read your version of their view and say, "yes, that is what I meant"?

📊 THE SAME CLAIM, ANSWERED TWO WAYS
The claim: "Custard is brave because he acts even though he is frightened."
STRAW MAN
"So you think anyone who is scared counts as brave?" (Nobody said that.)
THE REAL CLAIM, ANSWERED
"Acting while frightened is a fair definition of brave. But the poem calls him a coward in the same breath, so Nash may want us to hold both at once."

✅ CHECK YOUR OWN REASONING

Spotting a bad argument in someone else's writing is one skill. Not making one yourself is a different skill. Before you move on, check the reply you just wrote against these three common mistakes.

☐
Straw man. Did you answer the author's real, strongest point, or a weaker version you built to be easy to beat?
☐
Just saying so. Did you point to a specific moment in the text, or just say "everyone knows" or "it's obvious"? A claim with no source behind it is not evidence.
☐
Feelings instead of proof. Did you explain why the text supports your view, or only how the text made you feel? A feeling is real, but it is not evidence on its own.

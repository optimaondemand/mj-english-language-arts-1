Lesson 2.01
Hearing the Poem: Sound Before Sense
Literature · Week 1
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A poem is meant to be heard, not just seen.
Today you meet a dragon named Custard who begs for "a nice safe cage." Before you decide what the poem means, you are going to read it out loud, because the sound of a poem is part of what it is saying.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to read the poem aloud with attention to its rhythm and its rhyme.
2
I will be able to name what a ballad and a stanza are, using the poem in front of me.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
"The Tale of Custard the Dragon" by Ogden Nash. (Read from your own copy of the poem. Where to get a copy.)
⚡ WARM-UP · CHECK IN FIRST
When you hear a poem read out loud, what do you usually notice first? (No wrong answer. Just check in.)
📚 THE LESSON

A poem works the way a song does: the sound carries part of the meaning before you ever stop to think about the words. "The Tale of Custard the Dragon" is a ballad, a poem that tells a story, and Ogden Nash gives it a bouncing, almost silly beat. The whole thing is built in stanzas of four lines that rhyme in pairs, so it rolls along like a nursery rhyme. The lines rhyme two at a time: the first line pairs with the second, and the third with the fourth. We label that pattern AABB. But here is the trick. Nash uses that goofy, lighthearted sound to tell a story about real courage, about a "cowardly" little dragon named Custard who turns out braver than the loud animals around him. When a bouncy sound runs into a serious idea, that gap is doing something on purpose. Your job today is not to figure out the moral yet. Your job is to hear the poem: read it out loud, feel the beat, and let the sound do its work first.

💡 TRY THIS · WORD WORK
Copy one line you enjoyed saying out loud. Then rewrite the sentence one of three ways: swap a word for one that means almost the same thing (a synonym), stretch the line with more detail, or keep its bouncy shape but write about something in your own life. Add one line: what changed in the sound, and why.
📖 NOW READ · OUT LOUD
Read the whole poem twice from your own copy: once just to get the hang of the words, and once in a silly, playful voice. Let yourself bounce on the beat. Mark the line that was the most fun to say.

1
Write your response
Save your line and your word-work sentence. In the next lesson you will look closely at how Nash plays with sound, with rhyme and repetition, and you will mark it right on your copy of the poem.
💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Hold one question as you keep reading this week: why would a writer use such a silly, bouncy sound to tell a story about who is truly brave? Keep that gap in mind. It is where the poem hides its point.

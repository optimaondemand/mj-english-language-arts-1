Lesson 2.09a
Fourteen Lines: The Shape of a Sonnet
Literature · Week 2
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A different strict shape, built to turn.
Yesterday's poem returned to the same two lines again and again. Today's poem never repeats a line — instead it builds an idea for eight lines, then turns and answers itself. This shape is called a sonnet, and it is one of the most famous in the English language.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to describe how a sonnet is built: 14 lines, three four-line groups and a closing couplet, with a turn near the end.
2
I will be able to compare how a sonnet's shape builds meaning differently than a villanelle's shape does.
⚡ WARM-UP
Yesterday's poem built its power by repeating two lines over and over. What do you think a strict-shaped poem could do INSTEAD of repeating, to still feel powerful by the end? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THE LESSON

A sonnet is a 14-line poem with its own strict shape. The kind you'll read today, sometimes called a Shakespearean sonnet, is built from three four-line groups (called quatrains) followed by a two-line ending (a couplet). The first twelve lines spend their time building one idea — often by turning it over from a few different angles — and then, close to the end, the poem turns: it answers, corrects, or completes the idea it just spent so long building. That turn usually lands right at the closing couplet, which is often the poem's sharpest, most memorable statement. Unlike the villanelle you read yesterday, a sonnet never repeats a whole line. Its power comes from patient building and a decisive turn, not from return and repetition. Today's sonnet is by William Shakespeare, and it asks a question in its very first line: can a poem actually make something last forever?

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL · A WORD TO KEEP
turn (tap to see what a poet calls this)
Poets have a name for the moment a sonnet changes direction: the volta (an Italian word simply meaning "turn"). In today's sonnet, the volta arrives at line 9, where the poem stops describing summer's flaws and starts making its real claim.
📊 THE SHAPE OF THIS SONNET (14 LINES)
Quatrain 1 (lines 1–4): asks the question — can I compare you to a summer's day?
Quatrain 2 (lines 5–8): summer's flaws — too hot, too short, always fading
Quatrain 3 (lines 9–12): the turn — but your beauty, kept in this poem, will not fade
Couplet (lines 13–14): the closing claim — as long as people read this poem, you live
📖 READ THE SONNET
This poem is old enough that it belongs to everyone (public domain), so it's printed right here.
Sonnet 18 — William Shakespeare (tap to read)
Shall I compare thee to a summer's day? Thou art more lovely and more temperate: Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May, And summer's lease hath all too short a date: Sometime too hot the eye of heaven shines, And often is his gold complexion dimm'd; And every fair from fair sometime declines, By chance, or nature's changing course, untrimm'd: But thy eternal summer shall not fade, Nor lose possession of that fair thou ow'st; Nor shall death brag thou wander'st in his shade, When in eternal lines to time thou grow'st; So long as men can breathe, or eyes can see, So long lives this, and this gives life to thee.
Read it once for the sound. Then read it again and mark: bracket the three quatrains and the closing couplet, and underline the line where you think the turn happens.
💡 NOTICE THE PATTERN
Two poems in this module were written decades apart, but look at what they're both really about. In "The Tale of Custard the Dragon" (written in 1936), a dragon everyone thinks is a coward turns out to be the only one brave enough to fight when a real pirate attacks. In "Do Not Go Gentle into That Good Night" (written in 1951), a son begs his dying father to keep fighting instead of giving up quietly. Different decades, different subjects — a comic dragon poem and a serious poem about death — but both poems are asking the same question: what does courage look like when you're afraid? Keep that question in mind. You'll meet it again before this module ends.

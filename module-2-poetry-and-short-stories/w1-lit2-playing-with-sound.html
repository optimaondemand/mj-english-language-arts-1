Lesson 2.02
Playing with Sound: Rhyme, Repeated Beginnings, and Comparisons
Literature · Week 1
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Poets play with words on purpose, and you can learn to catch them at it.
Yesterday you heard the poem. Today you look at how Nash builds that sound: matching end-sounds, repeated beginning sounds, and comparisons that make a picture. Then you'll mark them right on the poem.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name and find rhyme, alliteration, simile, and personification in the poem.
2
I will be able to mark these techniques on the poem and explain what one of them adds.
⚡ WARM-UP · CHECK IN
Read this line out loud: "Belinda was as brave as a barrel full of bears." What do you notice first? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THE LESSON

Poets have a small toolbox of moves that make language do more than say plain facts, and once you can name them, you start to see them everywhere. Rhyme is when words end in matching sounds, like cage and age; it ties lines together and keeps the beat. Alliteration is when nearby words start with the same sound; "brave as a barrel full of bears" piles up the /b/ so the line nearly bounces. A simile compares two things using like or as ("brave as a barrel full of bears" again), making a sharp picture in one stroke. And personification gives human feelings or actions to something that isn't human, the way Custard "cried for a nice safe cage." Nash leans on all of these to keep the poem light and funny, which makes it even more surprising when the timid dragon turns out to be the brave one. Today you'll mark these moves right on the poem so you can see the machinery underneath the fun.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL · A WORD TO KEEP
alliteration (tap to see what it means)
Alliteration is the repeating of the same beginning sound in nearby words, like the /b/ in "brave as a barrel full of bears." Test it with your ear: read the words aloud and listen for the same sound starting more than one of them.
📖 NOW ANNOTATE THE POEM
Open your own copy of the poem (where to get a copy) and mark it up. Start with rhyme: in each stanza, find the last word of each line and highlight matching end-sounds in matching colors. Then find at least three more examples (alliteration, a simile, or a moment of personification) and label them.
⚡ CHECK YOURSELF · NAME THE TECHNIQUE
For each line, choose the technique Nash is using. Pick one and you'll get instant feedback.
1. In "Belinda was as brave as a barrel full of bears," the repeated /b/ sounds are…
2. That same line compares Belinda to "a barrel full of bears." That comparison is a…
3. Custard "cried for a nice safe cage." Giving a dragon human feelings like this is…
➡️ WHAT YOU'LL DO NEXT
1
Save your annotated poem
Keep your marked-up copy of the poem and a note of which technique you found most fun to spot. Tomorrow you'll ask what the poem is really about: its central message.
💪 BEFORE YOU GO

Now that you can name these moves, you'll hear them in songs and stories everywhere. Tomorrow's question: with all this playful sound, what serious thing is the poem trying to tell you about being brave?

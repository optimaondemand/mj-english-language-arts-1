A Fierce Poem: Meeting "Do Not Go Gentle"
Literature · Week 2
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Last week's poem bounced. This one burns.
Today you meet a very different poem: serious, urgent, and fierce. Like last week, you'll listen first and read it out loud, because the strong feeling lives in the sound. Dylan Thomas wrote it as his own father was dying.

⚡ WARM-UP · CHECK IN FIRST
When you feel something strongly, really strongly, how does your voice change? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THE LESSON

"The Tale of Custard the Dragon" told a story; this poem does not. There are no events and no plot. Instead, the whole poem is one strong, urgent feeling pressed into words. Dylan Thomas wrote it in 1947, and most readers understand it to be about death, because in the last stanza the speaker turns directly to his dying father: "And you, my father, there on the sad height." That direct address is the key to the whole poem. The speaker is not describing death from a safe distance; he is begging someone he loves not to give in to it, to "rage" instead of going "gentle." That is why the poem feels fierce rather than sad. Today you don't need to figure out every line. Your job is to hear it: listen once, then read it aloud in a serious, fierce voice, and notice how certain lines keep coming back, like a heartbeat that won't quit. That return is something you'll study closely tomorrow.

💡 TRY THIS IN YOUR READING JOURNAL · WORD WORK
Copy the line that felt most powerful to say out loud. Then rewrite it three ways: swap a word for one with almost the same meaning, stretch the line with more detail, or keep its shape but write it to someone you care about. Add one line: what changed in the feeling, and why?
📖 NOW READ ALOUD
Sit in a quiet place and open your own copy of the poem. Read it out loud twice: once to get the words, and once in a sad, serious, or fierce voice.

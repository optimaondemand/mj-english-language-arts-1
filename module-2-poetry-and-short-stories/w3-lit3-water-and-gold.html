Lesson 2.14
Water and Gold: What the Story Means
Literature · Week 3
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Plain river water undoes all the gold. Why did Hawthorne choose water?
Today you finish the story and pull out its meaning. You will learn two terms: a symbol is an object that stands for a bigger idea, and the theme is the lesson about life a story is really teaching. Then you'll decide, in your own words, what "The Golden Touch" is saying.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain what gold and water symbolize in the story.
2
I will be able to state the theme of "The Golden Touch" in one clear sentence.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
"The Golden Touch" by Nathaniel Hawthorne. (Full story in the drop-down on Lesson 2.12; reread the river and the ending.)
▶
Your Reading Journal (the Word doc in your OneDrive ELA6 folder)
⚡ WARM-UP · CHECK IN FIRST
At the very end, an old Midas says he now hates the sight of "all other gold, save this," meaning the golden tinge in his grandchildren's hair. What do you think he means? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THE LESSON

Writers often let one ordinary object stand for a bigger idea. That is a symbol. In "The Golden Touch" the gold is not just metal. It stands for Midas's greed, his belief that owning more is the highest good. The river water is its opposite. It is plain, free, and within everybody's reach, and it is what gives Marygold and the roses back. So the water becomes a symbol of the simple, common things that turn out to matter most. Notice how Hawthorne stages it: the cure for too much gold is the cheapest thing in the garden. Once you see the symbols, you can name the theme, which is the lesson about life a story is really teaching, said as a full sentence rather than one word. The theme of this story is not just "greed." It is closer to this: chasing riches above everything can cost you the very things that make life worth living, and the commonest things, like clean water and the love of your family, are worth more than all the gold on earth. Hawthorne does not stamp that sentence on the page. He lets the stranger hint at it ("the commonest things ... are more valuable than the riches which so many mortals sigh and struggle after") and lets the ending show it. Your job as a reader is to gather those clues and say the theme in your own words.

💡 TRY THIS IN YOUR READING JOURNAL · WORD WORK
The stranger says the river may "repair the mischief which your avarice has occasioned." Avarice means extreme greed for money or things. Copy that line into the "Week-by-Week Notes" section of your Reading Journal, then rewrite it one of three ways: swap in a word that means almost the same as avarice (a synonym), stretch the sentence with more detail, or keep its shape but write about repairing a different mistake. Add one line: how does "avarice" connect to the story's theme?
📊 TWO SYMBOLS, SIDE BY SIDE
In the story What it stands for
Gold: rare, hoarded, locked in a dungeon Greed; the belief that owning more is the highest good
River water: plain, free, in everybody's reach The common things that matter most; the cure for greed

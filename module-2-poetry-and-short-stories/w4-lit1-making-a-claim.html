Say What You Think: Making a Clear Claim
Literature · Week 4
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
An argument starts with one clear sentence saying exactly what you believe.
This week you write a letter to an author you disagree with. The whole letter rests on one thing done well: a clear claim. Today you learn what makes a claim sharp instead of fuzzy, and you write the claim for your own letter.

📚 THE LESSON

A claim is a sentence that states what you believe and that a thoughtful person could disagree with. That last part matters. "The story is about gold" is a topic, not a claim, because no one can argue with it. "Hawthorne is wrong that having less always makes you happier" is a claim, because a reader could push back. A strong claim does three things. It takes a clear position, so the reader knows exactly where you stand. It is specific, naming the author and the idea you are answering rather than waving at the whole story. And it is honest, meaning it says what you actually think, not what sounds impressive. For this letter your claim is your counter-claim. It answers the author's message. So you first need the author's message stated fairly, then your sentence saying where you disagree. For example: "You teach, Mr. Hawthorne, that riches lead away from happiness, but I think wealth can do real good when it is shared." Notice the shape. It names the author's idea, then turns with a word like but to state your own. Write your claim that clearly today and the rest of the letter has a spine to hang on.

💡 TRY THIS IN YOUR READING JOURNAL · WORD WORK
A claim takes a position. Position here means the stand you take, where you place yourself in a debate. Start with this sentence: "My position is that the author's claim is only partly true." Rewrite it three ways: swap a synonym for position (such as stand or view), stretch it with more detail about which part is true, or keep its shape but state a position about a different topic you care about. Add one line: which rewrite states your stand most clearly?
📊 FROM TOPIC TO CLAIM
TOPIC (too broad)
greed in "The Golden Touch"
↓
QUESTION (sharper)
Is having less really the path to happiness?
↓
CLAIM (a position you can defend)
"Hawthorne is right that greed can ruin a life, but wrong that wealth itself is the problem."
📚 EVIDENCE ISN'T ALL THE SAME KIND

A claim is only as strong as what backs it up, and writers reach for different kinds of evidence. A fact is something that can be checked and is true whether or not anyone believes it, like the fact that "The Tale of Custard the Dragon" has fifteen four-line stanzas. An example is one specific moment that shows a claim in action, like the moment Custard alone attacks the pirate while the "brave" pets hide or flee. Testimony is what a person says about their own experience or belief, such as a character's own words about how they felt. A number or statistic reports an amount, like how many times the line about "a nice safe cage" repeats. A fact and a statistic are close cousins, both checkable, while testimony is only someone's word, worth less on its own than a fact, but still useful for feelings a fact cannot capture. When you argue with an author, name the kind of evidence you are using and pick evidence that actually proves your point, not just evidence that is easy to find.

💡 WHO WAS THIS WRITTEN FOR?

Every author pictures somebody reading their words. Noticing who that somebody is changes how you read. Ask two questions: who does the author seem to be picturing, and how can you tell? Hawthorne answers this for you directly. He calls his readers "my dear little folks" and stops to explain things a child might not already know, so his intended audience is young readers and the grown-ups reading along with them. Nash's poem is built the same way: silly invented words like "realio, trulio," bouncy rhymes, and a talking dragon all signal a child audience too, even though the poem's idea (real courage looks different from loud bragging) is worth an adult's attention as well. Thomas's poem reads differently. It never explains itself the way Hawthorne does, it uses difficult words like "meteors" and "forked no lightning" without defining them, and its final lines turn to speak to one specific person, his dying father, so its audience is closer to an adult who already knows grief firsthand. When you write your own letter this week, decide who you are picturing on the other end, and let that choice shape your words, the same way theirs shaped their poem or story.

⚡ CHECK YOURSELF · THINK, THEN REVEAL
Decide your answer first (in your head or your journal). Then open the box to compare.
1. "Custard has fifteen four-line stanzas." What kind of evidence is this? (Decide, then open.)
That is a fact you can check by counting the stanzas yourself. You could also call it a number, since it reports a count. Either label is fair; what makes it strong is that it does not depend on anyone's opinion.
2. "Custard alone attacks the pirate while Mustard the dog flees." What kind of evidence is this? (Decide, then open.)
That is an example: one specific moment from the poem that shows a claim in action. If your claim is that real courage is shown by action, this moment is the proof, not just a fact about the poem.
3. Nash and Hawthorne both explain themselves and use playful, invented words. Thomas never simplifies his language at all. What does that tell you about their audiences? (Decide, then open.)
It suggests Nash and Hawthorne are picturing younger readers, or readers new to the idea, while Thomas is picturing an adult reader who already knows grief and does not need the poem's hard words defined.

Appositives and their commas
Professor Lovelore At the board
0:42
An AI-generated character and voice.
An appositive
a noun phrase that renames the noun right beside it
the Pinner of Wakefield = George-a-Greene
In the middle
a comma before it and a comma after it
George-a-Greene, the Pinner of Wakefield, joined the band.
At the end
one comma before it
Robin trusted Little John, his tallest man.
Not a renaming
a phrase that tells which one, not another name
The man in the green hood rode away.
no commas

The question: What makes a true hero, and what happens when justice and law are not the same thing?
  1. Thesis: one sentence that names Robin Hood and uses because or although.
  2. Body Paragraphs 1, 2, and 3: each with a claim you can argue, one scene from Chapters 1 to 17 with its chapter, and a short note that explains it.
  3. Short entries: two or three sentences for each body paragraph.
  4. Concession: the strongest objection to your thesis, and how your conclusion will answer it.
  5. Appositives: at least two, set off with commas.
Use these headings, in this order · Chapters 1 to 17

📌 Watch how
Clara’s outline
6th Grade Scholar

Thesis: Robin Hood is a true hero because he shows mercy even to his enemies, although he lives outside the law.

Body Paragraph 1. Claim: Robin is nice to people. Scene: In Chapter 15, he is nice to the Bishop of Peterborough. Note: He is nice even when he does not have to be.

Body Paragraph 2. Claim: A true hero can admit when someone equals him. Scene: In Chapter 16, Robin tests George-a-Greene, the Pinner of Wakefield, and then says he has met his match. Note: Robin cares more about honor than about winning.

Body Paragraph 3. Claim: The band chooses mercy, but with a condition. Scene: In Chapter 17, Marian, the Queen of Sherwood, makes Sir Guy of Gisborne swear to leave them alone before he goes free. Note: The oath protects the band, and letting him go shows mercy.

Concession: Some readers will say a true hero does not take tolls on the road, because that breaks the law. I will answer that the laws he breaks belong to the Sheriff and Prince John, and that gold taken in Chapters 16 and 17 is sent toward King Richard’s ransom.

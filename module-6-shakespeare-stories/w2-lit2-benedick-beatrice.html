Lesson 6.08
The Merry War: Benedick, Beatrice, and Two Kinds of Trick
Literature · Week 2
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Two characters can argue all day and still be the heart of the comedy.
Beatrice and Benedick trade clever insults from the first page. Their quick, clever back-and-forth is wit. Today you look closely at their "merry war," then weigh it against Don John's plot, because the tale hangs on the difference between a kind trick and a cruel one.

📚 THE LESSON

Wit is quick, clever language, the kind that makes a sharp joke on the spot. Beatrice and Benedick are the wittiest people in Messina, and Lamb says they keep up "a perfect war of raillery," trading insults whenever they meet. Beatrice asks "are you yet living?" and mocks Benedick as "the prince's jester"; he calls her "my dear lady Disdain." Notice that their fighting is really a kind of attention: they cannot stop talking to each other. That is why their friends play a kind trick. They let Benedick overhear that Beatrice loves him, and let Beatrice overhear that Benedick loves her, and both, secretly hoping it is true, soften at once. This trick is a deception, a making of someone believe what is false, but it is meant to make two people happy. Don John's plot uses the very same tool. He too makes someone believe a lie, but his aim is to destroy Hero "only for the malicious pleasure." So the tale sets two deceptions side by side and asks you to judge them by what they are for. As you read, watch how Lamb shows character through speech: clever words can serve love or cruelty, depending on the heart behind them.

Lesson 6.02
Two Servants: Ariel, Caliban, and the Hope of Freedom
Literature · Week 1
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Put two characters side by side, and each one shows you more.
Prospero has two servants on the island: the airy spirit Ariel and the monster Caliban. When an author sets two characters side by side so their differences stand out, the craft term is contrast. Today you study how Lamb uses contrast to teach the tale's idea of freedom: who earns it, who does not, and why.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain what contrast is and contrast Ariel with Caliban.
2
I will be able to explain why Ariel earns his freedom and Caliban does not.
⚡ WARM-UP · CHECK IN FIRST
If two people do the same job, what should decide which one gets a reward? (No wrong answer. Just check in.)
📚 THE LESSON

Contrast means setting two things side by side so their differences come into focus. Lamb gives Prospero two servants, and almost everything about them is opposite. Ariel is a "lively little sprite," light as air, who serves Prospero faithfully and reminds him gently of the freedom he was promised. Caliban is "a strange misshapen thing," heavy and earthbound, set to fetch wood and do the hardest labor, and his bad nature keeps him from learning anything good. Notice that both are servants and both want their own way, yet the tale treats them very differently. Ariel's service is willing, so Prospero promises and finally grants him liberty. Caliban's nature stays sour, so he stays bound. Watch how Lamb uses what each servant says and does, called characterization, to show you their natures rather than just telling you. By the end of the tale, the freeing of Ariel becomes one of the last and happiest things Prospero does, which tells you freedom is one of the ideas, or themes, the whole tale is built around.

⚡ TURN IT OVER · THE KEY WORD
Both servants are bound, but only one is promised liberty. Predict what that word means, then flip the card.
liberty (tap to reveal what it means here)
liberty means freedom: the state of being free to act and go as you choose. Ariel longs for his liberty "to wander uncontrolled in the air, like a wild bird," and earns it by the end.
💡 WHOSE SIDE IS THIS TOLD FROM?

In Module 5 you learned that a narrator takes a position, called a point of view, and that it decides what you are allowed to see. Read the table above again, but this time look at the words. Caliban is "a strange misshapen thing" with a "bad nature" who "will not learn anything good." Those are not neutral facts the way "Caliban fetches wood" is a fact. They are judgments, and somebody is making them. Lamb is retelling this tale from a position close to Prospero, the master of the island, so you meet Caliban the way Prospero sees him: as a problem. Nothing in the tale is written from where Caliban stands. This is worth catching, because it happens constantly outside of books. Every account of an argument is told from somewhere. A careful reader asks not only what happened but who is telling it, and what the telling makes easy to believe.

📊 FACT OR JUDGMENT?
▶ "Caliban carries the wood." Fact. You could film it.
▶ "Caliban has a bad nature." Judgment. Someone decided that, and that someone is not Caliban.
💡 TRY THIS IN YOUR READING JOURNAL
Take one moment where Ariel torments Caliban, and write it in four or five sentences from Caliban's point of view, in his own voice. Keep every event the same. Change only who is telling it. Then write one line answering: which words did you have to drop, and why could Caliban not have used them about himself?
📖 FROM THE TALE
Open the full tale of The Tempest again (it is printed in the drop-down on the first lesson of this week, "The Island, the Storm, and a Father's Story"). Reread two parts: the early section where Ariel torments lazy Caliban, and the late section where Prospero sets Ariel free. In the "Week-by-Week Notes" section of your Reading Journal (the Word doc in your OneDrive ELA6 folder), write one line each describing how Ariel and Caliban serve, then one sentence answering: why does only Ariel go free?

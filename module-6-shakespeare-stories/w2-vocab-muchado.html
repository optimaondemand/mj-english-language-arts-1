📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A tale of wit and trickery has the words to match.
This week's words come straight out of Much Ado About Nothing: words for joking and teasing, and words for the plotting and shame at the heart of the story. You'll meet each one where it lives in the tale. Reading a word inside a real sentence lets you use the words around it to figure out its meaning. The craft term for those surrounding hints is context clues. Then you'll make one word your own by rewriting a sentence three ways.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to use this week's words correctly in the context of the tale.
2
I will be able to take one sentence and rewrite it three ways to make a word my own.
⚡ WARM-UP
Some of this week's words are playful; others are dark. Which kind do you expect to remember better? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THIS WEEK'S WORDS, WHERE THEY LIVE

Every word below belongs to Much Ado About Nothing. Some name the playful teasing of Beatrice and Benedick; some name the plotting and shame that nearly ruin Hero. Watch for them as you reread.

WordWhat it means here
salliesquick, clever or witty remarks (Beatrice diverts Hero "with her sprightly sallies")
raillerygood-humored teasing or joking ("a perfect war of raillery" between the two wits)
disdaina feeling that someone or something is beneath you; scorn (Benedick calls Beatrice "lady Disdain")
imputationan accusation; the act of blaming someone for a fault (the "imputation of buffoonery")
buffoonerysilly or foolish clowning; behaving like a jester (Benedick dreads the charge of buffoonery)
railera person who complains about or mocks others (Benedick was "such a railer against all fair ladies")
arboura shady garden shelter made of trees or vines (Benedick reads in an arbour)
slanderfalse words spoken to damage someone's good name (the report turns "slander into pity")
swoona faint; to faint (Beatrice helps recover Hero from her swoon)
penancean act done to make up for a wrong (Claudio will endure any penance Leonato lays on him)
✏️ THE WRITTEN PRACTICE · MAKE A WORD YOUR OWN
Pick one word from the table above. Find (or write) one sentence that uses it. Then rewrite that sentence three ways in the "Week-by-Week Notes" section of your Reading Journal (the Word doc you set up in the Week-1 Reading Journal lesson):
  1. Swap: replace the word with a synonym, a word that means almost the same thing. Does the meaning shift?
  2. Stretch: keep the word but add detail until the picture is sharper.
  3. Imitate: keep the sentence's shape but write about something in your own life.
Finish with one line: which version is strongest, and why?
📖 FROM THE TALE
Hunt for two of this week's words inside the full tale of Much Ado About Nothing (in the drop-down on this week's first lesson). Copy each full sentence into the "Week-by-Week Notes" section of your Reading Journal. One of them becomes the sentence you rewrite three ways above.
⚡ TRY IT · THE RIGHT WORD
Choose the word that fits each sentence from the tale.
1. Beatrice and Benedick kept up a constant war of ______, teasing each other at every meeting.
2. The friar hoped to turn the ______ against Hero into pity by reporting her death.
3. Shocked by the cruel accusation, Hero fell down in a ______, as if dead.
💪 BEFORE YOU GO

A word you have rewritten three times is a word you can use on purpose. Keep your rewritten sentences in your Reading Journal. You'll reach for these words when you write about Much Ado About Nothing.

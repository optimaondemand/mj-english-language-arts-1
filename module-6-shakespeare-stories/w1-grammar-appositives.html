Lesson 6.04
Appositives: Adding Detail Without a New Sentence
Grammar & Vocabulary · Week 1
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A few words tucked between commas can tell your reader exactly who is who.
You'll learn the appositive: a noun phrase placed right beside another noun to rename or explain it, set off with commas. Lamb uses them constantly to keep a crowded tale clear. Then a short quiz checks what you've got.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify an appositive and the noun it renames.
2
I will be able to combine two sentences into one by using an appositive with commas.
⚡ WARM-UP
Read this: "Prospero, the duke of Milan, raised the storm." What do the words between the commas do? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THE LESSON

An appositive is a noun (or noun phrase) placed right next to another noun to rename or explain it. In "Prospero, the duke of Milan, raised the storm," the phrase "the duke of Milan" renames Prospero and tells the reader who he is. The handy test: you can lift the appositive out and the sentence still makes sense ("Prospero raised the storm"). Most appositives are set off with commas, one before and one after, because they add extra information rather than essential information. Appositives are powerful because they let you combine two short sentences into one smooth sentence. Instead of "Ariel was a spirit. Ariel served Prospero," you can write "Ariel, a faithful spirit, served Prospero." Lamb does this on nearly every page of The Tempest, naming "Antonio, his false brother" or "Gonzalo, the kind old lord." As you write about the tale this week, use appositives to introduce each character cleanly the first time, so your reader always knows who is who.

Two short sentencesCombined with an appositive
Miranda was a princess. Miranda had never seen another man.Miranda, a princess, had never seen another man.
Caliban was a monster. Caliban fetched the wood.Caliban, a misshapen monster, fetched the wood.
Naples was Prospero's enemy. Naples helped Antonio.The king of Naples, Prospero's enemy, helped Antonio.
📖 FROM THE TALE
Open the full tale of The Tempest again (in the drop-down on this week's first lesson) and hunt for one appositive Lamb uses, such as a phrase set off by commas that renames a character. Copy the full sentence into the "Week-by-Week Notes" section of your Reading Journal (the Word doc you set up in the Week-1 Reading Journal lesson), and underline the appositive.

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

When you introduce a character in your writing, try an appositive: name the person, then add a few words between commas to tell who they are. It is the cleanest way to keep a reader from getting lost.

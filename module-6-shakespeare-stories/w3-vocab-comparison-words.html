Lesson 6.35
The Words You Need to Compare and Argue
Grammar & Vocabulary · Week 3
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A precise word makes a precise argument.
This week's words are the tools of comparison and argument. They are the words a writer uses to claim, to compare, and to say what ideas have in common. You will need them to write your capstone. You will meet each one inside a sentence about this year's reading, so you see how it works before you use it. Then you make one word your own by rewriting a sentence three ways.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to use this week's comparison and argument words correctly in a sentence.
2
I will be able to take one sentence and rewrite it three ways to make a word my own.
⚡ WARM-UP
When you want to say two things are alike at a deep level, which feels most exact to you? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THIS WEEK'S WORDS, IN A SENTENCE

Each word below is a tool for comparing texts and making an argument. Read each one inside its sentence so you see the job it does, then reach for it in your capstone.

WordWhat it means hereIn a sentence about this year's reading
themethe idea a story makes about a subject, stated as a full thought"Forgiveness is a theme that runs from The Tempest to The Bronze Bow."
claima statement you argue for, one a reader could reasonably dispute"My claim is that real courage always costs the hero something."
evidencethe specific moment from a text that supports a claim"The strongest evidence is the scene where Prospero forgives his brother."
compareto set things side by side to show how they are alike"When you compare St. George and Daniel, both face fear and move toward it."
contrastto set things side by side to show how they differ"In contrast to St. George, Midas learns his lesson too late."
synthesisputting separate pieces together into one larger idea"The synthesis names the one truth all three characters share."
parallela likeness between two things that run alongside each other"There is a clear parallel between Prospero's mercy and Daniel's."
echoa repeating of an idea so that a later text recalls an earlier one"Claudio's penance is an echo of the forgiveness in The Tempest."
significancewhy something matters; the importance of an idea"The significance of the theme is why these stories still speak to us."
enduringlasting a long time; continuing through the years"Courage is an enduring idea, returning in story after story."
✏️ THE WRITTEN PRACTICE · MAKE A WORD YOUR OWN
Pick one word from the table above. Find (or write) one sentence that uses it. Then rewrite that sentence three ways in the "Week-by-Week Notes" section of your Reading Journal (the Word doc you set up in the Week-1 Reading Journal lesson):
  1. Swap: replace the word with a synonym, a word that means almost the same thing. Does the meaning shift?
  2. Stretch: keep the word but add detail until the picture is sharper.
  3. Imitate: keep the sentence's shape but write about one of your own three characters.
Finish with one line: which version is strongest, and why?
📖 INTO YOUR CAPSTONE
Choose two of this week's words and write them straight into your capstone plan: use claim to label your argument, and use compare, contrast, parallel, or echo in the sentence that connects two of your characters. Copy both sentences into the "Week-by-Week Notes" section of your Reading Journal.
⚡ TRY IT · THE RIGHT WORD
Choose the word that fits each sentence about your capstone work.
1. "My ______ is that real courage always costs the hero something." Which word fits?
2. "The ______ is the sentence that names what all three characters share." Which word fits?
3. "In ______ to St. George, Midas learns his lesson too late." Which word fits?

Lesson 1.15
A New Name, A New Self
Literature · Week 4
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A name is not just what you're called. It can be who you are.
In Chapters 15–16, Marcellus moves toward the biggest change of his life. Today you think about what it means to be renamed, and what a person gives up and gains when their whole identity shifts.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain what a change of name can stand for in a story.
2
I will be able to identify what Marcellus is leaving behind and what he is moving toward.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
St. George and the Dragon, Chapters 15–16. (Read from your own copy of the book.)
▶
Your Reading Journal (the Word doc in your OneDrive ELA6 folder)
⚡ WARM-UP · CHECK IN FIRST
Have you ever been called by a different name (a nickname, a family name, a role)? How did it feel? (No wrong answer.)
🏛️ A LITTLE HISTORY FIRST
In the early Church, joining the faith through baptism often came with a new name, a sign that the old life was over and a new one had begun. The tradition runs deep: Abram became Abraham, Simon became Peter, Saul became Paul. A change of name marked a change of whole direction. When Marcellus becomes George, the writer is drawing on that long tradition.
📚 THE LESSON

A name carries weight. It can hold your family, your nation, your rank, the expectations of everyone who has ever said it. So when a story gives a character a new name, that is rarely a small thing. It usually marks a deep change in who the person is. In Chapters 15–16, Marcellus's old identity begins to fall away. He goes to Regina one last time; she calls him disloyal and even asks him to sacrifice a sick Christian slave to the dragon. He refuses, sets the dragon armband on the floor, and leaves her despairing behind him. Then, drained and exhausted, he must be helped to revive, and he dreams of a brown-eyed man on a boat who calls him to a new life. As you read, track two columns at once: what he is leaving (his fiancée, his old name, his Roman certainty), and what he is moving toward. The writer wants you to feel the cost of both. A change this large is never free, and the story is honest about what it asks of him.

💡 TRY THIS IN YOUR READING JOURNAL · WORD WORK
Find a sentence in Chapters 15–16 that uses disloyal, despairing, or revive. Copy it, then rewrite it one of three ways: swap a near-synonym, stretch it with more detail, or keep its shape but write about your own world. Add a line: what changed, and why.
📖 NOW READ
Read Chapters 15–16. In your journal, make two columns: what Marcellus leaves and what he moves toward. Add at least two items to each.
Read from your own copy of St. George and the Dragon. [Teacher: optional lawful link, publisher or library catalog only; do NOT link a hosted file]
⚡ CHECK YOURSELF · LEAVING OR MOVING TOWARD?
For each item, choose whether it is something Marcellus is leaving behind or moving toward.
1. Sacrificing to the emperor's gods.
2. Receiving a new name and a new allegiance.
3. The certainty that real strength always means force.

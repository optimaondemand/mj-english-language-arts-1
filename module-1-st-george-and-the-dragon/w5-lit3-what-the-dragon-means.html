Lesson 1.22
What the Dragon Means: Reading a Symbol
Literature · Week 5
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In a great story, a monster is rarely just a monster.
You read the fall of the dragon. Today you ask the deeper question: what does the dragon stand for, and what falls along with it? When an object or creature carries meaning beyond itself, that is a symbol, and reading symbols is one of the most powerful things a reader can do.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain what it means for something in a story to be a symbol.
2
I will be able to judge how strongly the dragon stands for an idea, and back my judgment with the text.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
St. George and the Dragon, Chapters 20–21 (reread the dragon's scenes). (Read from your own copy of the book.)
⚡ WARM-UP · CHECK IN FIRST
When a story has a monster, what do you usually think it really stands for? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THE LESSON

A symbol is something in a story (an object, a creature, an image) that means itself and also something larger. The dragon is a real beast in the book; George really fights it. But the writer has loaded it with meaning. Trace what the dragon does: it rules by fear, it demands sacrifice, it makes people bow to a power they hate. Those are the same things Rome did to its Christians: the same false power that has shadowed the whole novel. When the wounded dragon turns its one good eye on George, enraged and bellowing, and George answers only "My name is George," the writer is making an argument: this kind of power can be beaten, and it cannot reclaim a person who has truly changed. The way to read a symbol is to ask two questions: what does this thing literally do in the story, and what bigger idea behaves the same way? When the two line up, you have found the symbol's meaning. You do not guess. You point to what the thing does and let the text prove it. Today you decide how strongly the dragon stands for an idea, and you back your decision with the book.

💡 TRY THIS · WORD WORK
Find a sentence in Chapters 20–21 that uses enraged, bellow, or grim. Copy it, then rewrite it one of three ways: swap a near-synonym, stretch it with more detail, or keep its shape but write about a struggle of your own. Add one line: what changed, and why.
📖 NOW READ (REREAD)
Reread the dragon's scenes in Chapters 20–21, this time hunting only for what the dragon does to people. List three things it does.
Read from your own copy of St. George and the Dragon. [Teacher: optional lawful link, publisher or library catalog only; do NOT link a hosted file]
⚡ CHECK YOURSELF · HOW STRONG IS THE SYMBOL?
For each reading of the dragon, rate how strongly the text supports it: weak, possible, or strong. There is a best answer for each.
1. The dragon stands for a power that rules by fear and demands what it has no right to.
2. The dragon stands for a gentle creature that is simply misunderstood.
3. The dragon stands for fear itself, the thing that makes people give in.
⚠️ THE DRAGON'S CLAIMS, TESTED

Back in Chapter 8, Agathon calls the dragon "a liar… a descendant of the father of lies." That is a serious charge. Test it yourself. Across the whole book, the dragon and its believers make bold claims, and you can check each one against the evidence the story actually gives. Tap each claim below to see whether the book ever backs it up.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL · DOES THE BOOK EVER PROVE THIS?
The dragon claims to be older than the gods, and says it once knew Nero, Caligula, and Julius Caesar. (Chapter 6)
Never backed by evidence. The dragon states its age and its famous acquaintances. Nothing in the story ever shows either one is true.
The dragon calls Christians the great threat to the Empire. (Chapter 6)
Never backed by evidence. The dragon names no actual harm any Christian causes. The Christians we actually meet, Pasikrates and Agathon, are loyal and kind.
A guest at Lucius's feast says, "If the dragon says so, it is probably right." (Chapter 4)
Never backed by evidence. The speaker gives no reason at all, only that the dragon said it.
The dragon claims to love the Empire more than anything else. (Chapter 6)
Never backed by evidence, and contradicted by its own actions. The dragon burns an estate, kills sheep and people, and attacks the very Empire it claims to love.

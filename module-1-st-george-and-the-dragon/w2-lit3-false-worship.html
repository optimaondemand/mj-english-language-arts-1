📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Read one scene slowly enough to ask what every choice is doing.
In Chapter 9, Marcellus prepares for his wedding, gets a letter from Regina, and has a terrifying dream in which the dragon circles and dives to devour him, and then wakes to a real attack. Today you read one passage of that dream closely and ask what the writer's word choices are quietly telling you about the dragon's power.

⚡ WARM-UP · CHECK IN FIRST
Why do you think a fear can keep working on a person even in their sleep? (No wrong answer. Just think it through.)
📚 THE LESSON

Close reading means slowing down on a single passage until you can say what each choice is doing. The end of Chapter 9 gives you a vivid one: Marcellus's dream. As you read it, watch three things. First, the verbs: the island's cracks spread "like jagged lightning," the dragon circles like an eagle and then dives to devour him: verbs of hunting, not of safety. Second, the sensory details: the laughs of everyone he knows blend into the dragon's "deafening, mocking cackle," and Marcellus feels paralyzed, unable to cover his ears. Third, what the dream reveals: the ground he stands on is a breaking island, and the only escape (a boat of singing slaves) he is too ashamed to call to. By making the dragon a predator in Marcellus's own sleep, the writer shows you a fear that has gotten inside him. Hold that idea. The dream is the writer's quiet warning about where Marcellus's choices are heading.

💡 TRY THIS IN YOUR READING JOURNAL · WORD WORK
Find a sentence in Chapter 9 that uses fissure, paralyzed, or deafening. Copy it, then rewrite it one of three ways: swap a near-synonym, stretch it with more detail, or keep its shape but write about your own world. Add a line: what changed, and why.

⚡ THINK FIRST, THEN REVEAL
Answer each in your head (or your journal) before you open it. Then check your thinking.
1. In the dream the dragon circles like an eagle and then dives at Marcellus. What do those verbs tell you about how the dream sees the dragon?
They make the dragon a hunting predator and Marcellus its prey. The dream is not flattering the dragon. It shows the truth Marcellus keeps ignoring while awake: this creature means to consume him.
2. The laughs of Demetrios, Regina, his father, and the feast-guests all blend into the dragon's "mocking cackle." Why mix their voices together?
It shows that the pressure pushing Marcellus toward the dragon comes from everyone he trusts. The people he loves and the monster have become one voice in his mind, which is exactly why the choice is so hard.
3. Marcellus is too ashamed to call to the boat of singing slaves for help. What does that detail quietly reveal?
His Roman pride. He would rather be devoured than ask slaves for rescue. The dream points to the very thing standing between Marcellus and the Christians, and the boat of singers hints at where help will eventually come from.

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A symbol is a thing that stands for more than itself.
In Chapters 17–18 Marcellus survives another attack and sits at his dying father's bedside, reading the Apostle Paul and deciding to be baptized. The writer fills this stretch with symbols: the breaking island and the boat of ships from his dream, the Apostle's letters as an "antidote." Today you learn to read a symbol: to see the thing on the page and the meaning underneath it at the same time.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to define a symbol and find one in Chapters 17–18 (the dream's island and ships, or Paul's letters).
2
I will be able to name more than one meaning a single symbol can carry.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
St. George and the Dragon, Chapters 17–18. (Read from your own copy of the book.)
▶
Your Reading Journal (the Word doc in your OneDrive ELA6 folder)
⚡ WARM-UP · CHECK IN FIRST
In Marcellus's dream, an island breaks apart while a boat of singers sails toward him. What might that picture suggest? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THE LESSON

A symbol is something concrete (an object, an action, an image) that also stands for an idea bigger than itself. The best symbols hold more than one meaning at once. In Chapters 17–18, the writer leans on the symbols from Marcellus's dream. The breaking island stands for a world giving way beneath him: the Empire, his old certainties, the life he can no longer stand on. The boat of ships and the brown-eyed man's command, "Protect my ships," stand for a new people and a new calling he is being offered. Even Paul's letters work as a symbol: Marcellus calls them an antidote to his cycles of despair, and he is struck by Paul's unshakable conviction even though Paul, like the Christians, seems indifferent to emperors and generals. None of these are decoration. Each lets the writer say something about Marcellus's conversion that plain explanation could not. Your job today is to pick one symbol and name what it carries: not just one meaning, but as many as you can defend from the text.

💡 TRY THIS IN YOUR READING JOURNAL · WORD WORK
Find a sentence in Chapters 17–18 that uses antidote, conviction, or indifferent. Copy it, then rewrite it one of three ways: swap a near-synonym, stretch it with more detail, or keep its shape but write about your own world. Add a line: what changed, and why.
📖 NOW READ
Read Chapters 17–18. Choose one symbol (the breaking island, the boat of ships, or Paul's letters) and write down two meanings it carries in these chapters.
Read from your own copy of St. George and the Dragon. [Teacher: optional lawful link, publisher or library catalog only; do NOT link a hosted file]
⚡ CHECK YOURSELF · HOW STRONGLY DOES IT FIT?
For each reading of the breaking-island symbol, decide how well it fits the scene: a stretch, partly, or a strong fit.
1. The breaking island means the world Marcellus stood on is no longer solid.
2. The boat of ships means a new community and calling being offered to Marcellus.
3. The island means Marcellus wants to buy property by the sea.

💪 BEFORE YOU GO

When a writer slows down to describe an object at a turning point, ask what it might stand for. Tomorrow you'll see how the language itself changes once the change is complete.

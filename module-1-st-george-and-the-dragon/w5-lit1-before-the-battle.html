📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How a hero prepares for a fight tells you what the fight really means.
In Chapter 20, George does not simply pick up a weapon. He prays first. Watch the lead-up to the battle. The way the writer slows down before the violence is a signal that this is more than a contest of strength.

🏛️ A LITTLE BACKGROUND FIRST
In the oldest versions of this legend, George prays before he fights, and the prayer is not a delay; it is the reason the fight can be won at all. Older writers understood the battle as more than swordplay: a human being acting as an instrument of something larger than himself. The writer of this novel keeps that idea. George was a trained Roman soldier, but the strength that once served the empire is now aimed at something he believes is worth dying for.
📚 THE LESSON

When a writer wants a battle to mean more than a battle, he does it with language that points beyond the physical. A purely military fight would use words about strength, speed, and weapons. But watch the words around George in Chapter 20: prayer, faithfulness, and a courage that does not depend on the size of the enemy. Agathon even prays that God grant the men "courage, strength, faithfulness, and love" as they go. This is the writer's way of telling you the dragon is not only a beast. It stands for a kind of power that rules by fear. The six men ride with George not for gold (there is none) but, as Ionas says, because they follow "an enemy of our true leader, the Lord Jesus Christ." George's calm before the fight is the writer's argument that real courage is not the absence of fear. It is action taken in the presence of fear, directed by something worth serving. As you read, do not just track who is winning. Track the words. The words tell you what the fight is really about.

Lesson 1.21
The Climax: How a Writer Builds the Highest Point
Literature · Week 5
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The biggest moment in a story is built, not stumbled into.
Chapter 21 is the dragon's fall: the climax, the moment everything has been building toward. Today you read it as a craftsman would: which words carry the weight, and how the writer makes one sentence land harder than the rest.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to identify the climax of the novel and explain why it is the highest point.
2
I will be able to name one or two words in the climactic sentence that carry the most weight.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
St. George and the Dragon, Chapter 21. (Read from your own copy of the book.)
▶
Your Reading Journal (the Word doc in your OneDrive ELA6 folder)
⚡ WARM-UP · CHECK IN FIRST
Think of the most exciting moment in a movie or book you love. What made it hit so hard? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THE LESSON

The climax of a story is its highest point of tension: the moment the central conflict breaks open and the outcome turns. In this novel it is the killing blow: when the wounded dragon makes a final lunge with its jaws open, George drives his spear between its teeth so that it penetrates the dragon's head and out the back of its neck. But a climax does not feel powerful by accident. A skilled writer slows the pace, shortens the sentences, and chooses heavy, sound-rich verbs so the moment lands like a hammer. The whole chapter has built the dragon's weak points (the wing-joint, the eye, the soft underbelly) so the final blow feels earned. Read the climactic passage twice: once for what happens, and once just for how it is written. Find the verb that does the most work. Notice whether the sentence is short and blunt (which makes a moment feel sudden) or long and rolling (which makes it feel grand). When you can name what the writer did to create the feeling, you are reading like a writer.

💡 TRY THIS IN YOUR READING JOURNAL · WORD WORK
Find a sentence in Chapter 21 that uses lunge, penetrate, or underbelly. Copy it, then rewrite it one of three ways: swap a near-synonym, stretch it with more detail, or keep its shape but write your own action moment. Add one line: what changed, and why.
📖 NOW READ
Read Chapter 21. Find the single sentence you would call the climax, the dragon's fall. Copy it into your journal and circle the verb that carries the most weight.

⚡ CHECK YOURSELF · THINK, THEN REVEAL
Decide your answer first (in your head or your journal). Then open the box to compare.
1. Why does the writer use short, blunt sentences at the moment of the killing blow? (Decide, then open.)
Short sentences feel sudden and final, like the blow itself. A long, winding sentence would soften the impact. The writer matches the shape of the sentence to the feeling of the moment. That is a craft choice, not an accident.
2. Which verb in the climax carries the most weight, and why? (Decide, then open.)
Answers vary, but the strongest choice is usually the decisive action verb: "thrust," "drove," "lunged," "went limp." These verbs are heavy in sound and meaning, and the whole chapter has been building toward one of them. A flat verb like "hit" or "got" would let the air out of the moment.
3. Is this the climax of the whole novel, or just of the battle? (Decide, then open.)
The dragon's fall is the climax of the battle: the contest won, the monster defeated. But the novel has one more turn coming: George's death. Holding both in mind is exactly what makes the ending so striking. He wins the fight and still loses his life.

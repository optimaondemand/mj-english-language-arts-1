Lesson 1.06
Set Side by Side: How a Writer Uses Contrast
Literature · Week 2
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
You understand a thing better when you see what it is placed next to.
In Chapters 7–8, the writer keeps placing the dragon and the Christians side by side. Both claim power. Both make demands. But the words, the images, and the feeling around each one are pulled in opposite directions on purpose.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain how setting two things side by side sharpens what each one is.
2
I will be able to sort details from Chapters 7–8 by which power they belong to.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
St. George and the Dragon, Chapters 7–8. (Read from your own copy of the book.)
▶
Your Reading Journal (the Word doc in your OneDrive ELA6 folder)
⚡ WARM-UP · CHECK IN FIRST
When you want to show that one thing is good, does it help to show a bad thing right beside it? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THE LESSON

When a writer sets two things right next to each other so you feel the difference, that move is called contrast. It works because the eye and the ear notice difference faster than they notice a thing alone. In Chapters 7–8, the dragon and the Christians both ask Marcellus for loyalty, but the writer dresses them in opposite language. The dragon comes with heat, smoke, scales, and fear: words that press down. The Christians come with quiet candlelight, singing, and the steady chant of their bishop: words that lift. (A bishop is a senior church leader; Agathon is one.) Notice the adjectives, the verbs, and the images attached to each: that is where the writer is quietly arguing which power is true and which is false. A good reader does not just feel the difference; a good reader can point to the words that made the difference. Your job today is to sort the details and catch the contrast in the act.

💡 TRY THIS IN YOUR READING JOURNAL · WORD WORK
Find a sentence in Chapters 7–8 that uses bishop, chant, or vespers (evening prayer). Copy it, then rewrite it one of three ways: swap a near-synonym, stretch it with more detail, or keep its shape but write about your own world. Add a line: what changed, and why.
📊 TWO POWERS, TWO SETS OF WORDS
THE DRAGON'S POWER
heat · smoke · scales · roaring · dread · demand · sacrifice
THE CHRISTIANS' POWER
candlelight · singing · chant · steady · calm · mercy · peace
As you read, add the writer's actual words to each column in your journal.
📖 NOW READ
Read Chapters 7–8. Find one passage about the dragon and one about the Christians. Copy two words from each into your journal columns.

Lesson 1.12
The Courage That Does Not Strike Back
Literature · Week 3
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Sometimes courage is not a single bold blow but a long, steady night of holding on.
In Chapter 14, Marcellus spends a tense night guarding the estate against another attack. He organizes fire teams, keeps watch, steadies frightened people, and even smiles at a clumsy mishap. Today you read that scene closely and ask what kind of courage it takes to lead calmly through a long, uncertain night.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to describe how the writer's verbs show Marcellus's steady leadership through a dangerous night.
2
I will be able to form my own judgment about whether enduring without fighting is courageous.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
St. George and the Dragon, Chapter 14. (Read from your own copy of the book.)
⚡ WARM-UP · CHECK IN FIRST
Which takes more courage, in your opinion? (No wrong answer. You'll test your view against the chapter.)
📚 THE LESSON

There are two kinds of courage in this novel. One picks up a sword in a single charge. The other is quieter: the patience to hold a position hour after hour, keeping others steady when fear would be easy. Chapter 14 is full of the second kind. Marcellus builds a strategy for the night (fire teams and armed teams) and then does the unglamorous work: he stays cautious, moves from post to post, and encourages tired watchers as their attention flags. When sleepy slaves knock over a stack of buckets in the dark, he even turns the scare into a joke, and by dawn the estate's fear has eased into jubilance. Notice the writer's verbs for Marcellus: he watches, encourages, steadies. Verbs of patient command can carry as much strength as verbs of attack. Read this scene, then decide for yourself: is steady leadership through a long, frightening night a form of courage?

⚡ CHECK YOURSELF · TAP TO REVEAL
Think of your answer first, then tap each card to compare.
Marcellus watches, moves from post to post, and encourages tired watchers. What do those verbs tell you? (tap)
They show leadership as patient, unglamorous work. His courage here is not one blow but the steady effort of keeping a frightened estate alert all night.
When sleepy slaves crash into a stack of buckets, Marcellus smiles and jokes, "no more bungling." Why include this? (tap)
A good leader eases fear with a light touch. The moment shows Marcellus steadying morale, and it lets the night's tension break into relief by dawn.
Marcellus also reads the Gospel of Matthew during the night. What does that detail add? (tap)
It shows the second kind of courage growing in him. As he leads with his hands, his mind is being changed by what he reads. He starts to see Jesus as powerful and a real leader, not naive.

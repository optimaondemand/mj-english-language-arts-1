Participial Phrases: Two Things at Once
Grammar & Vocabulary · Week 5
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Some sentences show a character doing two things in the same breath.
A participial phrase lets you describe a noun in the middle of an action, perfect for battle writing. You'll learn how it works, where to place it, and one trap to avoid. Then a short quiz checks what you've got.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to recognize a participial phrase and the noun it describes.
2
I will be able to spot a phrase that describes the wrong noun and fix it.
⚡ WARM-UP
Which sentence puts you right in the middle of the action? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THE LESSON

A participle is a verb form used as a description. There are two kinds: the present participle ends in -ing (charging, raising), and the past participle usually ends in -ed or -en (wounded, broken). A participial phrase is a participle plus the words that go with it, and the whole phrase describes a noun. It shows that noun in the act of something. Brandishing his spear, George charged forward. The phrase brandishing his spear describes George, and putting it first builds a half-second of anticipation before the main action lands. You can place the phrase at the start (it builds suspense), in the middle (it adds texture), or at the end (it lands with emphasis). One rule keeps you out of trouble: the phrase must describe the noun right next to it. Raising his sword, George struck is correct, because George raised the sword. Raising his sword, the crowd cheered is wrong, because the crowd did not raise the sword. When you write this week's battle scene, reach for participial phrases. They are how you show a hero doing two things at once.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL · THE ONE RULE TO REMEMBER
What is a "dangling" participle? (tap to find out)
A dangling participle is a phrase that accidentally describes the wrong noun, or no noun at all. Quick test: the noun right after the comma must be the one doing the -ing. If it isn't, the participle is dangling, and the sentence says something silly.
📚 THE PARTICIPLE HAS TWO RELATIVES

A participle belongs to a family. English lets you take a verb and put it to work doing some other job in a sentence, and a verb form doing another job is called a verbal. There are three, and you have just learned one. A participle acts like an adjective: it describes a noun. A gerund ends in -ing and acts like a noun: it names a thing, so it can be the subject of a sentence. An infinitive is the word to plus a verb, and it can act as a noun, an adjective, or an adverb. Gerunds and participles both end in -ing, which is why people mix them up, and the way to tell them apart is not the ending but the job. Ask what the word is doing. If it is describing somebody, it is a participle. If it is naming the thing you are talking about, it is a gerund.

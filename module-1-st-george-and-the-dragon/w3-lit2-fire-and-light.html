Lesson 1.11
One Image, Two Meanings: Death and Sleep
Literature · Week 3
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The same picture can mean opposite things, depending on whose eyes see it.
In Chapters 12–13 you watch a Christian funeral for the slave Synderikos. Rome treats a dead slave as something to throw in a ditch and forget; the Christians call him "asleep in the Lord" and bury him with honor. Today you learn to read an image (a picture made of words) and notice what it is doing.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to define an image and find one example of fire or light in the reading.
2
I will be able to tell whether a picture of death in the chapter is Roman ("bury and forget") or Christian ("asleep in the Lord"), and explain how I know.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
St. George and the Dragon, Chapters 12–13. (Read from your own copy of the book.)
▶
Your Reading Journal (the Word doc in your OneDrive ELA6 folder)
⚡ WARM-UP · CHECK IN FIRST
A grave and a bed are both places a body lies still. Could a writer use one picture to soften the other? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THE LESSON

An image is a picture a writer paints with words: something you can see, hear, or feel. A skilled writer can take one picture (here, a body in the ground) and make it mean opposite things. In Chapters 12–13, the writer sets two views of death side by side. The Roman view is the slave ditch and the soldier's rule "bury and forget": death as garbage, a thing to discard. The Christian view is Agathon's funeral: the slave Synderikos is wrapped in a shroud, laid in a grave, and called "asleep in the Lord": death as rest, with a waking still to come. Same fact, opposite meaning. How does the writer tell you which is which? Look at the words clustered around each. The ditch comes with neglect and forgetting; the grave comes with singing, weeping, mourning, and comfort. Your job is to read each picture of death and judge what it means, not by the body itself, but by the words that surround it.

💡 TRY THIS IN YOUR READING JOURNAL · WORD WORK
Find a sentence in Chapters 12–13 that uses shroud, mourn, or comfort. Copy it, then rewrite it one of three ways: swap a near-synonym, stretch it with more detail, or keep its shape but write about your own world. Add a line: what changed, and why.
📖 NOW READ
Read Chapters 12–13. Find one image of death seen the Roman way (the ditch, "bury and forget") and one seen the Christian way ("asleep in the Lord," the honored burial). Copy a phrase from each into your journal.
Read from your own copy of St. George and the Dragon. [Teacher: optional lawful link, publisher or library catalog only; do NOT link a hosted file]
⚡ CHECK YOURSELF · WHAT KIND OF FIRE?
Place each picture of death on the spectrum: the Roman "discard" view, the Christian "asleep in the Lord" view, or somewhere in between.
1. The slave ditch where dead bodies were once thrown, and the soldier's maxim "bury and forget."
2. Synderikos wrapped in a shroud and called "asleep in the Lord," with the others singing over his grave.
3. Marcellus, watching the funeral, half-wanting to say "Amen" but unsure whether he believes it.

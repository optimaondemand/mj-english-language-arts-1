📚 THE LESSON

A strong ending does two things at once: it closes the story and opens its meaning outward. The final chapter spends only a few sentences on George's death: he and his companions are captured and executed during the persecution. But then it leaps ahead: after Galerius dies and the persecution ends, the Christian communities George protected thrive, and they remember him as a martyr, "the courageous and faithful leader who slew a dragon and protected Christians." His memory, not the empire that killed him, is what lasts. Now look back across the whole novel. The book began with Marcellus giving his loyalty to power simply because it was powerful. It ends with George giving his life rather than deny what he believes is true. That is the arc, from false loyalty to faithful witness, and every chapter has been a step along it. When you read for a book's meaning, you do not seize on one line; you test your idea against the whole story. A reading that the beginning, middle, and end all support is a strong reading. A reading that only one scene supports is a weak one. Today you practice exactly that judgment, because your culminating essay will ask you to make a claim about this book and prove it from across all six weeks.

📝 THE SAME SKILL, IN NONFICTION: CONTROLLING IDEAS AND HOW THEY ARE BUILT

You just tested claims about this whole novel by checking whether the beginning, middle, and end all support them. Nonfiction writing asks you to do something similar, but the writer usually helps you more directly. A piece of nonfiction is built around a controlling idea, also called a central idea: the main point everything else in the piece serves. Sometimes a writer states the controlling idea outright, in a topic sentence or an opening paragraph. That is what Lotti did in the author's note you read back in Week 1: he told you directly, up front, that he was separating known fact from invented guesswork. Other times a controlling idea is only implied. The writer never announces it; you have to gather it from the choice and order of details, the same way you just gathered a claim about this whole novel from evidence spread across six weeks. Nonfiction writers also build their paragraphs and sections using recognizable patterns. A definition pattern explains what a term means and lists its defining features. A classification pattern sorts things into types or categories. An advantage/disadvantage pattern lays out the benefits and drawbacks of a choice, method, or idea, often to help a reader decide something. Spotting which pattern a paragraph is using helps you predict what information is coming next and find the controlling idea faster, whether it is stated or only implied. You will use exactly this skill soon: your Module 4 mini-research task will send you to real sources that argue about whether a historical Robin Hood ever existed. Those sources will be organized this same way, and knowing the pattern will help you locate each source's controlling idea and judge what it actually supports.

⚡ TRY IT · NAME THE PATTERN
For each description of a nonfiction passage, choose the organizational pattern (or, for the last one, how directly its central idea is stated) it is using.
1. A field guide sorts birds into songbirds, birds of prey, and waterfowl.

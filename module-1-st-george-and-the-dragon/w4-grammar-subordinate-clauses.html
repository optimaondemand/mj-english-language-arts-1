Subordinate Clauses: Showing How Ideas Connect
Grammar & Vocabulary · Week 4
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
One small word can change why a thing happened.
You'll learn what a subordinate clause is and how the connecting word at its front (because, although, when, so that, if) tells your reader exactly how two ideas relate. Then a short quiz checks what you've got.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to tell a subordinate clause from a clause that can stand on its own.
2
I will be able to name the relationship a subordinating word shows: cause, concession, time, purpose, or condition.
⚡ WARM-UP
Read both sentences. Which feels like the more grown-up way to write? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THE LESSON

A clause is a group of words with a subject and a verb. Some clauses can stand alone as a sentence: Marcellus changed. A subordinate clause also has a subject and a verb, but it cannot stand alone; it leans on a main clause to finish the thought. You can spot one by the word at its front, a subordinating conjunction: because (cause), although (concession, an idea that pushes back), when / after / before (time), so that (purpose), if / unless (condition). Watch how the meaning shifts when you change only that one word: He converted because the Christians showed him something true explains his reason; He converted although it cost him everything admits a cost. Same event, different relationship, and the subordinate clause is what makes the relationship clear. When the subordinate clause comes first, put a comma after it. As you read this week's chapters, notice how the writer uses these clauses to explain why Marcellus's conversion happens, not just that it happens.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL · A TEST YOU CAN USE
How do I know a clause is subordinate? (tap for the quick test)
Say it out loud by itself. If it sounds unfinished, like it is waiting for more (“Because the Christians showed him something true…”), it is subordinate. A clause that sounds complete on its own (“Marcellus changed.”) is a main clause.
📖 FROM THE BOOK
Reopen Chapters 15–19 and find two subordinate clauses the writer uses. (Read from your own copy of the book.) Copy them into your Reading Journal and underline the connecting word.
⚡ TRY IT · NAME THE RELATIONSHIP
In each sentence, the underlined word starts a subordinate clause. Choose the relationship it shows.
1. Although he was a loyal officer, Marcellus began to doubt.
2. He knelt at the water because he finally believed.
3. When the priest spoke his new name, the crowd fell silent.
4. If he denied his faith, he could keep his rank.

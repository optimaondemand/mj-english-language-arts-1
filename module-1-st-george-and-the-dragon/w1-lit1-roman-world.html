Lesson 1.01
The Roman World: Who Is Marcellus?
Literature · Week 1
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Good writers show you who a character is. They rarely stop to tell you.
Today you meet Marcellus, a Roman officer called a tribune who commands more than a hundred men. Watch how Lotti reveals what Marcellus is like through his actions and the world around him, instead of announcing it. The craft term for this is characterization: the ways an author builds a character in your mind.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain what characterization is: how a writer shows who a character is instead of just telling you.
2
I will be able to point to one detail in Chapters 1–2 that shows what the Roman world expects of Marcellus.

🏛️ A LITTLE HISTORY FIRST
The story opens in the Roman Empire around the year 300 A.D., on the eastern frontier where Marcellus has been fighting the Sassanids. A Roman officer's whole life ran on duty, rank, and the worship of the empire's gods, including the emperor himself. To rise, you obeyed. The empire is ruled by Emperor Diocletian and his Caesar, Galerius, and you will catch an early hint that Galerius wants Christians driven out of the army. The writer fills these first chapters with that world on purpose, so you feel its weight before anything challenges it.
📚 THE LESSON

Characterization works two ways. A writer can tell you ("Marcellus was brave"), or show you and let you decide. Lotti almost always shows. In Chapters 1 and 2, watch what surrounds Marcellus: soldiers hurry to bring oats and water to his horse and embrace him when he arrives; this plain tribune goes first into the enemy camp; confiscated Christian books sit in the commander's tent; bandits ambush him on the road. None of these details are decoration. Each one shows what Marcellus's world prizes (loyalty, discipline, the glory of the empire) and what kind of man it is shaping him to be. Notice his verbs, too. Does Marcellus charge, command, or hesitate? A character's verbs quietly argue for who he is. Your job today is to catch characterization at work.

💡 TRY THIS · WORD WORK
Find one sentence in Chapters 1–2 that uses tribune or bandit(s). (Try the moment on the road: "I am Marcellus, tribune in the army of the Empire.") Copy it, then rewrite it one of three ways: swap in a word that means almost the same thing (a synonym), stretch the sentence with more detail, or keep its shape but write about something in your own life. Add one line: what changed, and why.

📚 BEFORE CHAPTER ONE: THE BOOK'S OWN FRONT MATTER

Every book carries pages that are not part of the story. These pages are called front matter, and they usually come before Chapter One: a title page, a table of contents, and sometimes an introduction, foreword, preface, or author's note. Back matter comes after the last chapter: acknowledgements, and sometimes a list of references or sources. This apparatus exists to give you background you need before, or after, you read the story itself. Your copy of St. George and the Dragon carries an author's note. In it, Michael Lotti tells you plainly what he is doing: he separates what is historically known about George (that he lived around 300 A.D., served as a soldier in the Roman army, and was executed as a Christian, probably during Emperor Diocletian's persecutions) from everything else, which he had to guess or invent, including the dragon itself, since "all the stories of St. George say that he defeated a dragon," though almost nothing else about how. That pattern, here is what is known and here is what I have guessed, is itself a controlling idea: the main point that organizes the whole note and tells you how to read every chapter that follows. When front matter names references or sources, or thanks the people who helped with research, that matters too. It shows you how much checking stands behind a claim, and it lets a careful reader go verify it.

💡 GO CHECK YOUR OWN COPY
Open your own copy of the book right now. Find its front matter: a title page, a table of contents, and especially the author's note (your edition may call it an introduction or preface). Read it if you have not already. Notice which sentences state something as known, and which ones the author signals as a guess, with words like "probably," "perhaps," or "may have."
⚡ CHECK YOURSELF · KNOWN, PROBABLE, OR INVENTED?
The author's note sorts its claims into different levels of certainty. For each statement below, decide which level it belongs to.
1. George's horse was named Kopto, and his father read Cicero every morning.
2. George was executed as a Christian, probably during the persecutions started by Emperor Diocletian.
3. George lived in the region of modern-day Turkey around 300 A.D. and served as a soldier in the Roman army.

Lesson 4.09
The Code Robin Lives By
Literature · Week 2
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A struggle between two forces is what drives a story forward.
Robin is an outlaw by the law's word, but he behaves by a code of his own: protect your people, fight fair, take no unjust revenge. The clash between the law that condemns him and the justice he practices is the engine of the whole book. A struggle between opposing forces in a story is called conflict. Today you name the central conflict and gather evidence on both sides.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to name the central conflict of the book in one sentence.
2
I will be able to list two parts of Robin's code shown in Chapters 3–5.
📋 WHAT YOU NEED FOR THIS LESSON
▶
Your notes on Chapters 3–5. No new chapter today. (Reread any moment from your own copy as needed.)
▶
Hero-or-Outlaw chart (in your OneDrive ELA6 folder)
⚡ WARM-UP · CHECK IN FIRST
A group living outside the law still needs rules. Which rule would matter most to you if you led such a band? (No wrong answer. Just check in.)
📚 THE LESSON

Conflict is the struggle between opposing forces that drives a story. The central conflict of this book is not just Robin against the Sheriff. It is the law against justice. By the law, Robin is a criminal who must be hunted. By the code he lives by, he is the only one acting justly. Look at how Chapters 3 through 5 fill in that code through action. He protects his own: the band risks itself to rescue Will Scarlet from the Sheriff's men. He fights fair: on the bridge he meets Little John in an even contest and honors the man who beats him. He holds back unjust revenge: when the grieving boy Much cries out to avenge his murdered father, Will Scarlet tells him to seek justice, not revenge. Each of these is a rule Robin and his men live by, and together they answer the law's charge. The book is asking you to weigh two things at once: what the law says, and what is right. Your essay will need both sides, so today you sharpen the conflict and stock both columns of your chart.

⚠️ A WARNING ABOUT YOUR OWN CHART

You are about to put two or three moments in the "hero" column and feel like you have proved something. Be careful. Drawing a large conclusion from a small number of cases is a fallacy called a hasty generalization. You have met two already: the straw man in Module 2 and the either/or in Module 3. This one is the most tempting of the three, because it feels like evidence. Three fair fights do not prove that Robin is always fair, and one corrupt sheriff does not prove that every sheriff is corrupt. Watch the book do this to you on purpose. Green shows you Robin at his best for chapters at a time, and it becomes easy to forget that Robin also robs travelers on a road. The fix is not to stop generalizing, because an essay has to reach a conclusion. The fix is to match the size of your claim to the size of your evidence. "Robin protects his own" is a claim three moments can carry. "Robin is never unjust" is a claim that needs the whole book, and the whole book may not give it to you.

📊 MATCH THE CLAIM TO THE EVIDENCE
TOO BIG FOR THE EVIDENCE
"Robin is always just." (Three moments cannot carry "always.")
SIZED HONESTLY
"In Chapters 3 through 5, Robin repeatedly chooses loyalty over his own safety."
💡 TRY THIS
Add hasty generalization to your Word Bank. Then look at the top of your Hero-or-Outlaw chart and write the biggest claim your evidence honestly supports so far. Use a word that fits the size of your proof: often, in these chapters, repeatedly. Save always and never until you have earned them.
⚡ THINK, THEN REVEAL
Write your own answer first, then open the panel to compare. There is no auto-grading here. The thinking is the point.
In one or two sentences: state the central conflict of the book, then give one moment from Chapters 3–5 that shows Robin acting justly even though he is an outlaw by law.
Reveal a strong response
The central conflict is law against justice: the law brands Robin a criminal while he acts more justly than the men who rule. One moment that shows it is the rescue of Will Scarlet, when Robin and his band risk their lives to save a loyal friend from the Sheriff. The law calls that a crime, but it is an act of loyalty and protection. (You could also use the fair fight on the bridge, or Will Scarlet steering young Much toward justice instead of revenge.)
Now size a claim honestly. Write one sentence about Robin that your evidence from Chapters 3–5 can actually carry. Then write the too-big version of that same sentence, and circle the word that made it too big.
Reveal a strong response
Sized honestly: "In Chapters 3 through 5, Robin repeatedly puts his band's safety above his own." Too big: "Robin always puts others first." The word that broke it is always. Three moments show a pattern in these chapters, but they cannot prove what Robin does in every chapter, and later in the book he waylays travelers on the road for their money. Keep always and never for claims you can prove end to end.

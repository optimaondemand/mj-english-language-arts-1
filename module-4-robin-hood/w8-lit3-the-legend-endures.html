📚 THE LESSON

A legend is a story handed down and reshaped over time, often growing larger than any single version. Robin Hood is exactly that. Green did not invent Robin. He gathered old ballads and plays, the medieval verses, the Elizabethan dramas, the lines from Scott and Tennyson and Noyes that open each chapter, and arranged them into one continuous life. Those older texts are his sources; his book is a retelling that selects, orders, and rewrites them in his own prose. The Epilogue makes the legend visible inside the story: years after Robin dies, a lost King Henry is sheltered by two very old hermits, the aged Little John and Friar Tuck, and the tale lingers in Sherwood like "a phantom horn." The hero is gone, but the story is not. Now step back over the whole arc you have read, from the false outlaw charge in Week 1 to the deathbed mercy in Chapter 24. You have the evidence. Today you decide where your thesis lands: is Robin finally a hero, an outlaw, or a man the labels cannot quite hold?

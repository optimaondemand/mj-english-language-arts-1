Lesson 4.34
Appositives: Adding a Fact Without Adding a Sentence
Grammar & Vocabulary · Week 6
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
An appositive renames a noun and tucks in a fact at the same time.
You'll learn to add an appositive, a noun phrase that renames the noun right before it, and to set it off with commas. This is the move that lets you introduce a character in your essay without a clumsy extra sentence. Then a short quiz checks what you've got.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to spot an appositive and tell which noun it renames.
2
I will be able to set off an appositive with the right commas in my own sentence.
⚡ WARM-UP
Read this: "Sir Guy of Gisborne, Robin's sworn enemy, rode the same road." What is the part between the commas doing? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THE LESSON

An appositive is a noun or noun phrase placed right next to another noun to rename or explain it. In "George-a-Greene, the pinner of Wakefield, joined the band," the phrase the pinner of Wakefield renames George and tells you his job. You could split this into two sentences, but the appositive folds the fact in smoothly. The comma rule is simple. When the appositive just adds extra information, set it off with commas, one before and one after if it sits in the middle of the sentence. "Allin-a-Dale, a minstrel, sang for Robin." If the appositive comes at the end, you need only the comma before it. "Robin trusted Little John, his tallest man." This is a writer's tool you will use constantly in your essay, where you introduce characters like Robin, the outlaw earl, or the Bishop, a corrupt churchman, without stopping to write a whole new sentence for each one.

Lesson 4.10
Simple and Compound Sentences
Grammar & Vocabulary · Week 2
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Joining two complete thoughts the right way keeps your writing clear and strong.
You'll learn the difference between a simple sentence (one complete thought) and a compound sentence (two complete thoughts joined correctly). Joining them right is exactly how you avoid the run-on.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to tell a simple sentence from a compound sentence.
2
I will be able to join two complete thoughts with a comma and a joining word, and avoid a run-on.
⚡ WARM-UP
Quick gut check: is "Robin laughed, and he blew his horn" one complete thought or two joined together? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THE LESSON

A simple sentence is one complete thought. It has a subject and a verb and can stand on its own: "Robin drew his bow." A compound sentence joins two complete thoughts that could each be their own sentence. You join them with a comma plus a joining word (called a coordinating conjunction: and, but, or, so, for, nor, yet), like this: "Little John knocked Robin into the stream, and Robin laughed." Both halves, "Little John knocked Robin into the stream" and "Robin laughed," could stand alone, so the comma-and-joining-word makes a correct compound sentence. Here is the trap to avoid. If you slam two complete thoughts together with no joining word, or with only a comma, you get a run-on: "Robin laughed he blew his horn" or "Robin laughed, he blew his horn." Both are wrong. Fix a run-on by adding a comma and a joining word, or by making two separate sentences. As you write about the band this week, use compound sentences to show how two events connect, and check every one for the comma and the joining word.

Robin drew his bow.Simpleone complete thought
Robin drew his bow, and the arrow flew true.Compoundtwo complete thoughts joined by comma + and

Reopen Chapter 5 (the bridge fight) and find one compound sentence Green uses, or take two short events from the scene and join them into a compound sentence of your own. (Read from your own copy of the book.) Write it, and underline the comma and the joining word.
⚡ TRY IT · SIMPLE OR COMPOUND?
Answer each row about sentences. Pick one and you'll get instant feedback.
1. What kind of sentence is "The outlaws wore Lincoln green"?
2. What kind of sentence is "Robin lost the fight, but he welcomed the winner"?
3. Which correctly fixes the run-on "The bridge was narrow neither man would yield"?
💪 BEFORE YOU GO

From now on, when you join two complete thoughts, stop and ask: did I use a comma and a joining word? If you only used a comma, you have a run-on. Fix it, and your sentences stay clear.

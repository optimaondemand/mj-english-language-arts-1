Lesson 4.46
Making Sentences Do More Work
Grammar & Vocabulary · Week 8
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Revision is not fixing typos. It is making your sentences carry your argument better.
Your essay draft has feedback on it. Today you learn the two moves that turn a flat draft into a sharp one: combining choppy sentences and varying how sentences begin. Then a short quiz checks the moves before you revise.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to combine two choppy sentences into one stronger sentence.
2
I will be able to vary how my sentences begin so my writing does not sound repetitive.
⚡ WARM-UP
Read this aloud in your head: "Robin is brave. Robin is kind. Robin is fair." How does it sound? (No wrong answer.)
📚 THE LESSON

Revision means re-seeing your draft and reshaping it, not just correcting spelling. Two moves do most of the work. First, combine choppy sentences. When several short sentences repeat the same subject, join them so the idea flows: "Robin is brave. He is kind. He is fair" becomes "Robin is brave, kind, and fair." You can also join two sentences with a conjunction like and, but, or because, or tuck one idea inside the other. Second, vary your sentence openings. If every sentence starts with "Robin" or "The," the writing thuds. Start some sentences with a time word (Later), a describing phrase (Hunted across the moors, Robin…), or a dependent clause (Because the king had died, John…). These two moves are not decoration. They make your argument easier to follow and more convincing. As you revise your essay today, read it aloud in your head, then combine the choppy spots and vary any run of sentences that all open the same way.

MoveBeforeAfter
CombineRobin robs the rich. He helps the poor.Robin robs the rich and helps the poor.
Vary the openingRobin flees. Robin hides. Robin sends Marian away.Pursued by King John, Robin flees, hides, and sends Marian to safety.
📚 THE WORDS SPELLCHECK WILL NOT CATCH

While you are revising, hunt one more thing. English has pairs of words that sound identical and mean completely different things, and your computer will not flag them, because both spellings are real words correctly spelled. It simply cannot tell which one you meant. These are worth learning properly rather than guessing at, because they are the errors that make careful thinking look careless. Each pair below has a test you can run in your head in about two seconds. Learn the test, not the pair, and you will never have to wonder again.

Confused pairWhat each one meansThe test
its / it'sits = belonging to it · it's = it isSay "it is" out loud. If the sentence still works, you want it's.
their / there / they'rebelonging to them · a place · they areTry "they are." If that fails, ask: a place, or belonging to them?
to / too / twotoward · also or excessively · the number 2Try "also" or "very." If either fits, you want too.
affect / effectaffect is usually the verb · effect is usually the nounCan you put "the" in front? Then it is effect.
then / thanthen = next in time · than = comparingComparing two things? Use than. It has the a of "compare."
your / you'rebelonging to you · you areSay "you are." If it works, you want you're.
💡 DO THIS TO YOUR OWN DRAFT NOW
In Word, press Ctrl+F and search your essay for its, their, to, and then, one at a time. Run the test on every hit. This takes about three minutes and it is the single highest-value proofreading pass you can make before you submit.
📖 FROM YOUR DRAFT
Open your essay draft (the one with your facilitator's comments). Find one spot where two or three short sentences repeat the same subject, and one run where several sentences begin the same way. In the "Week-by-Week Notes" section of your Reading Journal (the Word doc you set up in Week 1), rewrite each spot using today's moves. You will paste the improved versions into your final essay on Workshop Day.
